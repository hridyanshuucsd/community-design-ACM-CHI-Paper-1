### WeDesign: Generative AI-Facilitated Community Consultations for Urban Public Space Design
- **Authors:** (2025 CHI paper; team incl. researchers from Concordia/Montréal-based urban planning HCI group)
- **Venue/Year:** CHI 2025 (also arXiv:2508.19256) 2025
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19256
- **Relevance:** Directly named related work. Custom platform integrating Stable Diffusion XL for real-time image generation in a half-day community consultation workshop (Montréal, 5 mixed focus groups of residents/designers/AI specialists) plus 6 interviews with planning professionals. Claims real-time generative images stimulate creativity and iterative idea exchange, but surfaces limits (poor depiction of accessibility/mobility needs, local architectural cues, bilingual prompting) and recommends open-source tooling with region-specific in-painting, multilingual support, group voting, and structured facilitation, directly motivates the proposed tool's emphasis on realism, zoning-compliance, and legitimacy rather than purely evocative imagery.

**Methodology summary:** WeDesign uses a single-site, mixed-methods qualitative study: one half-day, in-person community consultation workshop in Montreal (no experimental conditions/control group, a single deployment of the custom WeDesign platform, which layers a prompt-based UI and group voting/facilitation features over Stable Diffusion XL for real-time text-to-image generation of urban public space concepts) plus a separate round of six semi-structured interviews with urban planning professionals not present at the workshop. The workshop was organized into five focus groups mixing residents, urban designers/architects, and AI specialists, with dedicated note-takers per group. Participants iteratively wrote text prompts, generated and discussed images, and reacted/voted on outputs; the platform logged prompts, generated images, and engagement signals (reactions, turns). Analysis combined manual qualitative coding (open coding, iteratively refined codebook, Cohen's kappa reliability check on a 20% subsample, remainder single-coded) of transcripts and field notes with LDA topic modeling of the interviews, workshop notes, and the ~440 prompts (used descriptively, not as a primary finding-generating method, given corpus size), triangulating the two to derive themes about participation, inclusion barriers, and design recommendations. There is no pre/post quantitative outcome measure, survey instrument, or statistical hypothesis test, the study is a single-shot, exploratory qualitative deployment study, not a controlled comparison.

**Strengths:**
- Strong, deliberate diversity/inclusion in recruitment of the resident sub-sample (age, disability/wheelchair users, LGBTQ+, racial/religious minorities, bilingual speakers), directly enables the paper's central claims about equity and accessibility gaps in generative-AI consultation tools, which a CHI reviewer would likely praise as going beyond convenience-sampled 'residents.'
- Mixed qualitative + computational-text triangulation (manual coding with reliability checks vs. LDA topic modeling across three distinct corpora) gives convergent validity to themes rather than resting on a single analyst's interpretation.
- Real-world, real-stakeholder deployment (actual urban planners, architects, and residents in an actual Montreal consultation context) rather than a lab study with proxy participants, giving strong ecological validity for a civic-design tool.
- Honest and specific limitations section (representativeness, language/facilitation bias, expectation-inflation risk) rather than boilerplate disclaimers, and concrete mitigations reported (honoraria, flexible scheduling, watermarking outputs, explicit framing as non-binding).

**Weaknesses:**
- Single deployment, single city, single half-day session with no comparison condition (no baseline non-AI consultation, no repeated sessions over time), a CHI reviewer would flag this as a case study/proof-of-concept rather than an evaluation that can support causal or comparative claims about generative AI's benefit over status-quo consultation methods.
- Small, non-representative, self-selected sample (29 workshop participants, 6 interviewees) drawn from a single city, explicitly acknowledged by the authors as limiting generalizability; the '14 residents' cell is itself split across 5 focus groups, meaning very few residents per group to generalize sub-group patterns from.
- Inter-rater reliability was computed on only 20% of the coded data, with the remaining 80% coded by a single author, a common but reviewer-noticeable weakening of qualitative rigor, since most of the corpus lacks any reliability check.
- LDA topic modeling on a small corpus (single workshop's transcripts/notes and ~440 short prompts) is a fairly weak computational method for this scale of data; the authors themselves hedge it as a 'descriptive lens' rather than a validated model, which is appropriate but also signals the computational analysis adds limited rigor beyond the qualitative coding.
- No quantitative outcome or attitude measures (e.g., no pre/post satisfaction, sense-of-legitimacy, or perceived-influence scales) despite the paper's motivating claims about legitimacy and empowerment in civic design, leaves the core 'does this feel like real influence over decisions' question purely inferential from qualitative impression.
- Facilitation asymmetry (residents could converse in their preferred language but were pushed to prompt in English for the SDXL system) is a confound the study surfaces but does not control for, potentially conflating 'AI can't render local/bilingual cues' with 'workshop protocol forced English prompting.'

**Sample size / conditions:** Single condition/single deployment (no experimental manipulation or control group). Workshop: 29 participants total, 14 community residents (deliberately diverse: multiple age groups, 2SLGBTQ+ members, wheelchair users, racialized/religious minorities, French/English/bilingual speakers), 5 computer/AI scientists, 5 urban architects/designers, 5 note-takers, organized into 5 mixed focus groups for one half-day session in Montreal. Separately, 6 semi-structured interviews were conducted with urban planning professionals who did not attend the workshop. Output volume: ~440 prompts, ~1,760 generated images (4 per prompt).

**Measures used:** No standardized psychometric scales or Likert-type quantitative instruments. Measures were: (1) qualitative, workshop transcripts, facilitator field notes, and semi-structured interview transcripts, analyzed via open/thematic coding with a Cohen's kappa inter-rater-reliability check on a 20% subsample; (2) computational-text, LDA topic modeling (k swept 5-12) over interviews, notes, and prompts as a descriptive triangulation layer, plus word-cloud analysis of prompt vocabulary; (3) platform telemetry, counts of prompt submissions, image generations, 'heart' reactions/votes, and speaking-turn counts per participant/group, used as engagement indicators rather than formal dependent variables.

**Supplementary material notes:** The arXiv version (2508.19256) functions as the fullest available account; no separate appendix/supplementary PDF was found distinct from the main text, the methodological detail (participant breakdown, Cohen's kappa on 20% subsample, LDA k-sweep, engagement telemetry, and the itemized limitations) is embedded in the paper body itself rather than in a separate supplement.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite it in the main paper's related work, since it is the closest prior deployed system tackling generative-AI-mediated civic design consultation and its documented failure modes (poor accessibility/mobility depiction, weak local architectural fidelity, bilingual prompting limits) directly motivate why the proposed tool prioritizes zoning-compliant, geometrically grounded 3D co-design over purely evocative 2D image generation.

**Cite in supplementary:** Yes, its methodological precedent belongs in supplementary materials as a design-study exemplar: specifically, its practice of reporting Cohen's kappa on only a 20% coding subsample plus LDA-as-descriptive-triangulation is a useful, citable precedent for how our own study can justify a similarly lightweight-but-transparent qualitative rigor standard for a comparably small, resource-constrained multi-stakeholder workshop deployment, and its 'watermark outputs, frame as conversation starters not approvals' mitigation for expectation-inflation is a concrete precedent worth adapting for our own IRB/ethics-adjacent design discussion of avoiding false legitimacy claims from AI-generated community proposals.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Sample: 29 workshop participants (14 residents including wheelchair users, 2SLGBTQ+ members, racialized/religious minorities, bilingual French/English speakers; 5 computer scientists; 5 urban architects/designers; 5 note-takers), plus 6 semi-structured interviews with urban planning professionals, in a single half-day workshop in Montreal.
- Data volume: ~440 text prompts generated, 4 images per prompt (~1,760 total generated visualizations), plus workshop transcripts, field notes, and platform engagement logs (submissions, 'heart' reactions, speaking turns).
- Analysis: open coding of interview/workshop transcripts with iterative codebook, Cohen's kappa inter-rater reliability computed on only 20% of the data (remainder single-coded), triangulated with LDA topic modeling (k=5-12) applied separately to interviews, workshop notes, and prompts, used explicitly as a 'descriptive lens' given the small corpus rather than a definitive model.
- Authors' stated limitations: self-selected/non-representative sample despite outreach and honoraria; 'modest sample size and single-city context' limiting generalizability; facilitation/language bias (residents spoke in preferred language but prompted in English); risk of AI visuals inflating community expectations, mitigated via watermarking and framing as 'conversation starters, not design approvals.'
- Key finding relevant to related work: generative images broadened participation and enabled iterative idea exchange but struggled to depict accessibility/mobility needs, local architectural cues, and bilingual prompting, motivating recommendations for open-source tooling, region-specific in-painting, multilingual support, group voting, and structured facilitation.

### CoDesignAI: An AI-Enabled Multi-Agent, Multi-User System for Collaborative Urban Design at the Conceptual Stage
- **Authors:** (2026 preprint)
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv 2026 (arXiv:2603.16008) 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16008v1
- **Relevance:** Directly named related work. A round-based, multi-agent multi-user system where AI agents facilitate/participate in conceptual-stage collaborative urban design alongside human stakeholders. Authors note current limitation: AI agents cannot yet actively lead discussion or orchestrate multi-role interaction, a gap the proposed voice-first co-design tool targets by having AI facilitate zoning-aware design generation directly in a shared 3D scene rather than only messaging.

**Methodology summary:** CoDesignAI (arXiv:2603.16008v1, Zhang, Wu, Ren, Turaga & Mendel, March 2026) is a systems/design paper, not an empirical study. It presents the architecture of a web-based, multi-user, multi-AI-agent tool for conceptual-stage urban design: an AI Facilitator agent that synthesizes group input round-by-round (only after all users have contributed, to preserve fairness), plus on-demand Designer/Planner expert agents that respond reactively to individual queries, integrated with generative image AI and spatial/mapping services for street-level visualization. The paper's only demonstration of the system is a single hypothetical scenario walkthrough with two fictional residents ('Resident A' and 'Resident B') illustrating a multi-round design-revision workflow. There is no participant recruitment, no experimental conditions, no quantitative or qualitative measures, and no statistical or coding analysis -- the authors explicitly frame the work as prototype/design exploration and state a pilot study with real design experts and community participants is future work.

**Strengths:**
- Clear, well-motivated systems contribution: articulates a concrete architecture (AI Facilitator + on-demand expert agents + generative/spatial visualization + multi-round revision with documentation) that other researchers can build on or compare against.
- Honest and specific about its own limitations rather than overclaiming -- explicitly names the exact capability gap (AI cannot lead discussion or orchestrate multi-role interaction) instead of glossing over it, which is unusually self-aware for a systems paper and useful to cite.
- The round-based turn structure (facilitator waits for full participation before synthesizing) is a thoughtful, explicit design choice aimed at procedural fairness, a legitimate HCI design contribution independent of empirical validation.
- Positions itself clearly within a trajectory (single-agent -> multi-agent, single-user -> multi-user LLM-supported participatory design) making its contribution easy to place in a related-work map.

**Weaknesses:**
- No human evaluation whatsoever: zero participants, zero conditions, no measures -- a CHI reviewer would flag this immediately as failing to meet the venue's evidentiary bar for a system claiming to support 'collaborative' and 'participatory' design; the core interaction claims are entirely untested.
- The sole illustrative scenario uses fictional, author-authored personas (Resident A/B) rather than real users, so even as a case study it cannot surface real conversational breakdowns, misunderstandings, or facilitation failures -- a classic 'demo-ware' critique.
- No comparison condition (e.g., vs. a human-only facilitator, vs. single-agent baseline, vs. asynchronous commenting tools like the developer-proposal status quo) so no claim of AI facilitation adding value over alternatives is testable.
- Scalability, a claim central to the paper's motivation ('achieving efficient and scalable citizen engagement'), is explicitly acknowledged as unevaluated -- a direct tension between the paper's stated problem and its actual contribution.
- Reliance on external online/API access for spatial mapping and generative components is noted as a constraint but not stress-tested, leaving real-world deployment robustness (latency, connectivity, cost) unaddressed.
- No discussion of legitimacy/authority questions -- i.e., whether AI-synthesized 'shared design intentions' would be accepted by real stakeholders or policymakers as a legitimate alternative to expert-authored proposals, which is exactly the legitimacy question central to our own paper's contribution and is left entirely open here.

**Sample size / conditions:** None. No human participants were recruited and no experimental conditions were run. The only 'demonstration' is a single fictional two-resident (Resident A / Resident B) walkthrough scenario used to illustrate the workflow, not a study.

**Measures used:** None. No quantitative metrics, surveys, or qualitative coding schemes are reported, since no data collection occurred.

**Supplementary material notes:** Recommend a short supplementary note (not a measure to reuse, since none exist) citing CoDesignAI as a cautionary methodological precedent: it is a systems/HCI paper with zero human participants, zero conditions, and no measures -- its entire 'evaluation' is one fictional two-resident walkthrough scenario -- and it explicitly flags this as a limitation, promising a future pilot study with 'design experts and community participants.' This is useful precedent for our supplementary methods section as (a) a contrast case justifying why our study needs real participant sessions/measures rather than a walkthrough, and (b) evidence that the field currently lacks empirical baselines for multi-agent co-design facilitation, strengthening the novelty claim of any user study we do run. Its round-based, fairness-motivated turn-taking design (AI facilitator waits until all users have spoken before synthesizing) is also worth noting in supplementary as a design pattern precedent (not an empirical measure) relevant to our own turn-taking/voice-floor-control mechanism.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes -- cite it in the main related-work section (already planned), but frame it precisely as a system/architecture contribution rather than an empirically validated one, since our paper can credibly claim the harder, evaluated step: this is the paper that most directly anticipates our multi-agent, multi-user, AI-facilitated co-design premise, and its own stated limitation (AI agents cannot yet lead discussion or orchestrate multi-role interaction) is the exact gap our voice-first, in-scene AI facilitation targets -- but the sentence should not imply CoDesignAI was user-tested, since it wasn't.

**Cite in supplementary:** 

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "Rather than proposing a finalized solution, this study centers on design exploration through the development of a prototype."
- "At this stage, however, the system does not enable AI expert agents to actively guide or lead the discussions, coordinate interactions across roles, or participate in more advanced message orchestration."
- "We have proposed only a system prototype based on a relatively simple setting, and we have not yet examined how conversational dynamics may vary across different real-world contexts."
- "Scalability is not empirically evaluated in the current work."
- "A next step of this research is to work with design experts and community participants to conduct a pilot study."
- AI Facilitator agent only synthesizes group discussion 'after all users have contributed their input' (a round-based turn structure used specifically to preserve fairness/prevent AI dominance of discussion).
- Designer/Planner expert agents are invoked reactively, 'on demand,' only in response to individual participant queries -- they do not proactively intervene.
- The system's only demonstration of use is a single hypothetical/fictional walkthrough scenario ('Resident A' and 'Resident B') in Section 4, not a real deployment or user study.

### BoundarEase: Fostering Constructive Community Engagement to Inform More Equitable Student Assignment Policies
- **Authors:** Cassandra Overney, et al.
- **Venue/Year:** Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction (CSCW 2025) 2025
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3710938
- **Relevance:** Directly named related work. Sociotechnical, mobile-friendly web platform co-designed with a ~150,000-student US school district letting community members explore, simulate, and give feedback on proposed attendance-boundary maps to make an opaque, polarizing civic process more transparent and participatory. Relevant precedent for map/policy-simulation interfaces that let non-experts reason about tradeoffs in a legitimate, high-stakes civic decision, analogous to letting residents reason about zoning tradeoffs in building co-design.

**Methodology summary:** Two-phase field study run in partnership with a real US public school district (~150,000 students) undergoing an actual, live high-school attendance-boundary redistricting affecting 10,000+ families. Phase 1 (formative): semi-structured interviews with 16 parents who had participated in the real redistricting process; participants spent ~20 minutes exploring the district's existing ArcGIS-based boundary map, gave feedback on a real proposal, and reflected on the engagement process. Transcripts were analyzed via affinity diagramming (iterative clustering, team discussion, input from a district director) to surface design requirements that directly shaped BoundarEase's design. Phase 2 (evaluation): semi-structured think-aloud interviews with 12 parents (6 overlapping with the formative sample) who first ranked the district's four official policy pillars (feeder patterns, home-to-school distance, SES diversity, school utilization), then walked through the BoundarEase prototype sequentially, address input, interactive current/proposed boundary map, visualizations/text explaining policy impacts, 5-star rating of proposals, open-ended written feedback, reaction to a GPT-3.5-generated ("synthetic") student-perspective vignette, and an optional revision of their rating/feedback, while verbally answering Likert-scale questions comparing BoundarEase to the district's status-quo engagement process.

**Strengths:**
- High ecological validity: embedded in a real, live, high-stakes redistricting process with a real 150k-student district and real policy pillars/data, not a synthetic lab task, exactly the kind of 'legitimate alternative in a real civic process' framing our own paper needs to be judged against.
- Sound two-phase (formative → evaluative) design: the formative study's affinity-diagrammed findings visibly and traceably shaped the evaluation prototype's features, giving a clear needs-finding-to-design-to-evaluation chain that CHI/CSCW reviewers reward.
- Mixed-methods rigor with transparent qualitative process: two-level hierarchical codebook, described collaborative-then-independent coding procedure, and a reported inter-rater reliability statistic (Cohen's Kappa = 0.68), many HCI qualitative papers omit this, so its inclusion is a methodological strength reviewers would likely praise.
- Honest, specific limitations reporting: authors explicitly flag the data-agreement timing problem (using proxy/purchased data instead of the district's authoritative data) and explicitly name the synthetic-persona feature as a temporary stand-in for real community voice rather than quietly presenting it as finished/validated.
- Participant overlap across formative and evaluation studies (6 of 12) allows some longitudinal/before-after insight into how the same real stakeholders' views evolved as the tool matured.

**Weaknesses:**
- Very small, single-site sample (16 formative / 12 evaluation participants, one district, one redistricting case), a reviewer would flag limited generalizability to other districts, other policy domains, or larger/more diverse communities.
- Recruitment bias toward already-engaged, advantaged participants: both samples were pulled largely from the district's existing Student Assignment Team/parent working group and skewed heavily female (15/16, 11/12) and toward higher-SES families (11/16, 8/12), exactly the demographic most civic-engagement tools claim to need to reach beyond, which undercuts the paper's own equity framing and is a natural reviewer critique.
- No control condition or counterbalanced comparison: 'BoundarEase vs. status quo' comparisons are entirely retrospective self-report Likert items gathered in the same session immediately after using the new tool, which invites novelty/demand-characteristic bias and cannot isolate the tool's effect from the effect of simply being interviewed and walked through a polished demo.
- The signature 'authentic community voice' feature (synthetic student perspectives) was actually GPT-3.5-generated fiction standing in for real students/parents at evaluation time, a reviewer could reasonably ask whether participants' trust/legitimacy ratings for this feature are meaningful or measuring reactions to fabricated content, which is a validity concern specific to AI-mediated 'community voice' features (directly relevant to any AI-generated content in our own tool).
- Coding reliability was established between only two research-team coders (not an independent, naive third-party coder), and Kappa=0.68 is 'substantial' but not 'almost perfect' agreement by conventional Landis & Koch bands, worth noting rather than treating as unambiguously strong.
- No outcome data on real-world impact: the paper does not report whether use of BoundarEase changed the actual board's redistricting decision, shifted meeting attendance/polarization, or had any measurable downstream effect on the real policy process it was embedded in, evaluation is limited to self-reported perceptions during a single lab-like walkthrough session.

**Sample size / conditions:** Formative study: n=16 parents (15 female/1 male; 11 higher-SES, 5 medium-SES areas; spanning 14 of 40 affected schools; recruited via parent working group and general community). Evaluation study: n=12 parents (11 female/1 male; 8 higher-SES, 4 lower-SES areas; 9 actively vs. 3 moderately/less involved in the real rezoning; 6 overlapped with the formative sample). Recruitment for both drew heavily on the district's existing Student Assignment Team / parent working group, i.e., already-engaged parents. No control group or randomized/between-subjects condition: the evaluation is a single-group, within-subject design comparing BoundarEase to the status quo via retrospective self-report and Likert items administered verbally during the same session as the prototype walkthrough, not a counterbalanced or between-subjects comparison. $30 Amazon gift cards used as incentive in both studies.

**Measures used:** Qualitative: interview transcripts + researcher notes, analyzed via thematic analysis with a two-level hierarchical codebook (15 parent codes, 38 child codes) built by two coders who first collaboratively coded 3 interviews to refine the codebook, then independently coded the remaining 9, resolving disagreements via discussion; inter-coder reliability reported as Cohen's Kappa = 0.68. Quantitative: verbally-administered Likert-scale items comparing BoundarEase to the status-quo process (e.g., reported ~100% of participants (12/12) rated BoundarEase's explanation of individual/community impacts as better than status quo; ~82% agreed it helped them understand impacts on others; ~92% (11/12) found alignment with the four policy pillars clearer than before); a 5-star rating scale for proposed boundary plans; and tabulated distributions of how participants ranked the four policy pillars.

**Supplementary material notes:** The paper references (but the fetched text does not reproduce verbatim) supplementary materials containing: the full interview protocols/questions for both the formative and evaluation studies, the complete two-level codebook (15 parent / 38 child codes), and a GitHub release of 'all of the software, algorithms, and data.' These were not independently retrievable in this pass (PDF page-rendering tools were unavailable in this environment), so exact verbatim protocol wording and full codebook contents could not be directly quoted, only what secondary extraction of the HTML/PDF text surfaced.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the main related-work section, in one sentence, because BoundarEase is the closest existing precedent for a legitimate, district-sanctioned civic simulation/mapping tool that lets ordinary residents reason concretely about tradeoffs (SES diversity vs. distance vs. utilization) in a real, high-stakes, polarizing policy process, which is structurally analogous to letting community members reason about zoning/building tradeoffs as a legitimate alternative to a developer's proposal.

**Cite in supplementary:** Two methodological details from BoundarEase are worth carrying into OUR supplementary materials as precedent: (1) their qualitative rigor practice, a two-level hierarchical codebook plus reported Cohen's Kappa (0.68) from two-coder independent coding after a joint calibration pass on a subset of interviews, as a template for how we should report our own coding reliability; and (2) their explicitly flagged limitation that an AI-generated 'community voice' feature (their GPT-3.5 synthetic student personas) is a validity-limited placeholder for authentic stakeholder input, not equivalent to it, a cautionary precedent directly relevant if our own tool uses AI-generated content/personas/summaries to represent community members, and one we should preempt in our limitations/methods appendix.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "The research team met synchronously to cluster the initial topics to form categories, which we refined into a two-level hierarchical codebook."
- Cohen's Kappa = 0.68 for independent coding of the remaining 9 evaluation interviews.
- "Unfortunately, the signed data agreement with the school district came following the conclusion of the project. Therefore, datasets for school populations, school attendance boundaries, and travel times were drawn from both public and purchased datasets" (rather than the district's own authoritative data).
- "In later iterations, we envision replacing these synthetic [GPT-3.5-generated student] perspectives with actual input from students and parents", an explicit acknowledgment that the tool's 'community voice' feature was an AI-generated stand-in, not real, at evaluation time.
- Roughly 100% (12/12) and ~92% (11/12) of the small evaluation sample rated BoundarEase's explanatory/pillar-alignment clarity above the status quo, but these are self-report Likert comparisons collected in the same session as the demo, not independent pre/post or field-deployment measures.
- Both formative (n=16) and evaluation (n=12) samples were recruited substantially through the district's existing Student Assignment Team/parent working group and skewed heavily female (15/16 and 11/12) and toward higher-SES areas (11/16 and 8/12), i.e., already-engaged, relatively advantaged parents rather than a representative cross-section of the affected community.

### Value-Centered Framing Supports Inclusive Idea Convergence in Participatory Civic Design
- **Authors:** Jeongeon Park, Irene Hou, Risa Sundu, Steven P. Dow
- **Venue/Year:** ACM Collective Intelligence Conference (CI 2025) 2025
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3715928.3737470
- **Relevance:** Directly named related work (Dow co-authored). Shows that grounding participatory civic design discussions in an explicit shared value (rather than open brainstorming) improves inclusive convergence on ideas and participants' understanding of why choices were made, directly informs how the proposed tool should structure group deliberation/voting around competing design options in real time.

**Methodology summary:** Within-subjects lab experiment (n=24) using a static, non-functional interactive HTML/CSS/JS prototype simulating a civic voting task for a real, ongoing San Diego county recreational-park planning project (ideas pre-seeded from that live civic project; no participant input actually affected outputs -- a design probe, not a live deployment). Three counterbalanced conditions manipulate how much personal-value reflection precedes voting: (1) OnlyVoting -- vote on 3 randomly shown ideas, view overall popularity; (2) Value -- first pick 1 of 5 values (then a sub-value/scale item) derived from the Recreation Experience Preference (REP) framework, then vote on 3 ideas within that value category, view popularity broken down by value; (3) Idea+Value -- same as Value but preceded by an open-ended personal idea-generation step before value selection. Every participant did all three conditions in one of several randomized/counterbalanced orders (order effects tested via Friedman + repeated-measures ANOVA and found non-significant, then dropped). After each condition participants completed a survey with six 7-point Likert items (Mental Effort, Confidence, Inclusion, Alignment, Community Understanding, Willingness to Compromise) plus condition-specific open-ended questions; the study closed with a brief structured exit interview asking which condition participants preferred, felt most involved in, and best understood community opinions through.

**Strengths:**
- Clean, well-controlled within-subjects design with proper counterbalancing and an appropriate non-parametric statistical pipeline (Friedman + Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon) matched to Likert data and small n
- Grounds the ad-hoc 'value' construct in an established, validated psychometric instrument (REP scale) rather than inventing categories from scratch, and documents the abstraction/tagging process transparently (including using an LLM as a tagging aid with manual review)
- Reports a null result honestly and uses it as a selling point (no significant increase in mental effort) rather than only reporting significant effects -- addresses an obvious potential confound (does adding steps just add friction?)
- Mixed-methods triangulation: quantitative Likert results are corroborated with matched qualitative quotes for each measure, and the qualitative material also surfaces genuine boundary cases/dissenting voices (P9, P10, P12, P15) rather than only confirmatory quotes
- Uses a real, live civic dataset (an ongoing San Diego county park engagement project) for ecological grounding of the stimuli rather than fully synthetic ideas
- Transparent, itemized limitations section (sample demographics, static/non-live prototype) and full survey instrument + value taxonomy published in an appendix, aiding replication

**Weaknesses:**
- Very small, homogeneous, convenience sample (n=24, largely university-affiliated/student, recruited via social/group chats) with no non-expert demographic diversity (age, income, education, tenure/homeownership) -- a serious external-validity concern for a paper about civic representativeness, which a CHI reviewer would flag hard given the topic is literally about inclusion of diverse community voices
- The prototype was a static, non-functional mock-up: participants' votes, ideas, and value selections were never actually reflected in what they saw (pre-scripted 'other participants' results) -- so all effects are participants' reactions to a scripted simulation of social proof, not real group dynamics, real stakes, or real consequence -- a threat to ecological validity the authors themselves flag but likely undersells
- All measures are single-item, self-report Likert constructs with face-valid but unvalidated wording (not a published/validated multi-item scale for constructs like 'inclusion' or 'alignment'), so measurement reliability (no Cronbach's alpha, since single items) and construct validity are unclear; a CHI reviewer would ask why not use existing validated scales (e.g., procedural justice, sense of voice) or multi-item constructs
- No inter-rater reliability, coder count, or codebook reported for the qualitative deductive coding -- coding was apparently done post hoc by the same research team with categories chosen from their own hypotheses, raising risk of confirmation bias in theme selection
- One-shot, single-session exposure with no measure of durability/decay of the value-framing effect over repeated real-world engagement (novelty effect confound is plausible and unaddressed statistically, though anecdotally discussed in discussion)
- Order effects were tested with parametric repeated-measures ANOVA layered onto a nonparametric (Friedman-based) design and reported only as 'non-significant, thus omitted' without full statistics -- underspecified reporting of a secondary analysis
- The three conditions differ in more than the theoretical variable of interest: Idea+Value adds an entire extra idea-generation task, meaning any effect vs OnlyVoting is confounded with task length/investment, not purely 'value framing' per se (partially acknowledged in Discussion 5.1 but not tested statistically, e.g. via a controlled 'extra-step-without-values' condition)

**Sample size / conditions:** n=24 (12 women, 10 men, 1 non-binary, 1 prefer-not-to-say); recruited via student group chats/social media in the surrounding university community; 4 of 24 had prior urban-design/city-planning/community-project experience. Three within-subjects conditions: OnlyVoting (baseline majority voting, no values), Value (value selection before voting), Idea+Value (open-ended personal idea generation + value selection before voting). Counterbalanced order across the three conditions.

**Measures used:** Six 7-point Likert-scale self-report items administered after each condition: Mental Effort, Confidence, Inclusion ("my ideas are meaningfully considered"), Alignment ("community-suggested ideas reflect my personal values and priorities"), Community Understanding ("helps me better understand the opinions of the community"), and Willingness to Compromise ("even if my idea is not implemented, I would still be willing to use the park"). Plus condition-specific open-ended survey questions and a structured exit interview. The value taxonomy itself (5 domains, 21 scale items) was adapted from the validated Recreation Experience Preference (REP) scale (Manfredo, Driver & Tarrant 1996), narrowed from Schwartz's 10 universal values (deemed too abstract for lay users) via ChatGPT-assisted tagging of real community ideas, manually filtered by the research team -- full item list given in Appendix A.2.

### VizCrit: Exploring Strategies for Displaying Computational Feedback in a Visual Design Tool
- **Authors:** Mingyi Li, Mengyi Chen, Sarah Luo, Yining Cao, Haijun Xia, Maitraye Das, Steven P. Dow, Jane L. E
- **Venue/Year:** CHI 2026 (arXiv:2603.04754) 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04754
- **Relevance:** Directly named related work (Xia and Dow co-authored). Between-subjects study (N=36) comparing textbook-based, awareness-centered, and solution-centered computational feedback for novice visual designers; solution-centered feedback reduced design issues and increased self-perceived creativity. Directly relevant to designing how the AI assistant should surface zoning-compliance feedback (awareness vs. concrete-fix framing) to non-expert co-designers in the 3D tool.

**Methodology summary:** A controlled lab study manipulating one factor, actionability of AI-generated design-critique feedback (textbook text vs. awareness annotation vs. solution annotation), across three between-subjects arms, with a single shared design-revision task, counterbalanced across two seed designs, followed by mixed quantitative (rubric-scored issue counts, expert creativity ratings, CSI self-report, pre/post learning tests, telemetry) and qualitative (thematic-coded interviews) outcome measures, analyzed with non-parametric tests appropriate to small unequal-variance cells.

**Strengths:**
- Clean, well-controlled between-subjects manipulation of a single theoretically meaningful factor (actionability spectrum: text → awareness → solution) with a plausible mechanism and clear operationalization of each condition.
- Triangulates multiple outcome types, behavioral/telemetry, expert-rated artifact quality, validated self-report instrument (CSI), pre/post learning test, and qualitative interviews, rather than relying on a single self-report measure, which lets them catch the self-report/expert-rating divergence.
- Correct statistical choice (Kruskal-Wallis + Bonferroni-corrected Mann-Whitney) given small, likely non-normal cell sizes, rather than defaulting to ANOVA.
- Uses independent expert raters (blind to condition, presumably) for both issue-counting and creativity rating, reducing self-report bias for the key design-quality outcome.
- Counterbalances seed design against condition, controlling for a known confound (task/content effects).

**Weaknesses:**
- N=36 across 3 conditions (~12/cell) is small for a between-subjects design with multiple DVs; likely underpowered for interaction effects or subtler differences, and a reviewer would flag the risk of Type II errors especially for the null results (learning gains, degree of change, expert-rated creativity).
- Single task, single domain (graphic/visual design principles, not spatial/architectural or civic domain), single session (novice, one-shot exposure), limited ecological and temporal validity; authors themselves flag this.
- Sample is a homogeneous novice convenience sample (SONA undergrads, mostly women, narrow age range) with no non-expert 'community stakeholder' or multi-stakeholder groups, a real gap for anyone wanting to generalize to civic co-design contexts.
- No inter-rater reliability statistic reported (e.g., Cohen's kappa) for the expert rubric scoring of design issues or creativity, despite this being a central dependent variable scored by only 2 raters who may not be blind to condition given they likely built the system.
- It's a single-user, individual-revision task, no multi-user/collaborative condition, so the actionability-spectrum finding has not been tested in a group co-design or negotiation setting, which is the exact gap the current project needs to fill.
- The self-perceived-creativity vs. expert-rated-creativity divergence is a genuinely important and somewhat under-examined tension: the paper reports it but doesn't deeply probe why solution-centered feedback inflates self-perception without improving actual creative output (risk of over-trusting AI 'fixes').

**Sample size / conditions:** Between-subjects design, N=36 (28F, 7M, 1 prefer-not-to-say; age 18-35, M=21.4), recruited via UCSD SONA. Nearly all self-identified as novices in visual design (one intermediate). ~12 participants per cell across 3 conditions × 2 seed designs (counterbalanced). Conditions: (1) Textbook-based, static text principle definitions, no situated context; (2) Awareness-centered, visual annotations highlighting violations without prescriptive fixes; (3) Solution-centered, visual annotations flagging issues in red with green arrows suggesting concrete fixes. Sessions 60-90 min: demographics → pre-test (vocab + principle-application) → tool tutorial → main revision task on a shared seed design (one violation per principle) while thinking aloud → post-test (identical to pre-test) → Creativity Support Index (CSI) survey → semi-structured interview.

**Measures used:** Quantitative: CSI subscales (overall creativity, enjoyment, results-worth-effort, 5-pt Likert); expert ratings of final design creativity (10-pt scale, 2 design-expert raters); count of remaining principle violations via a 4-point rubric per principle (2 expert raters); pre/post-test learning gain (vocabulary + principle application); "degree of change" metric across image/graphic/text/background dimensions; behavioral logs (Firestore) of principle-panel click frequency/timing and iteration counts; feedback-accuracy rating (0/0.5/1) on 3 randomly sampled annotations per participant per phase (early/mid/late). Qualitative: reflexive thematic analysis (3 coders, inductive+deductive, Condens platform) of interview transcripts; coding of whether participants passively accepted vs. independently modified suggested fixes. Stats: Kruskal-Wallis H omnibus tests, Mann-Whitney U post-hoc with Bonferroni correction, α=.05/.01.

**Supplementary material notes:** Appendices (per project site/arXiv HTML) include: design-probe co-design examples used to derive the annotation designs, a comprehensive table explaining all annotation/feedback types shown to participants, reference materials detailing the specific design principles used in the rubric, and system implementation details (OCR processing pipeline, issue-detection algorithm specifications, Firebase/Firestore database architecture for logging). Useful as a template for how to document our own compliance-detection algorithm and annotation taxonomy in our supplementary material.

**Cite in main paper:** Cite in the main paper's related-work section as direct empirical precedent (and co-authored by Xia/Dow) showing that concrete-fix ('solution-centered') AI feedback improves compliance/issue-resolution and perceived creativity/satisfaction over abstract awareness-only feedback, directly motivating the design choice to surface zoning-compliance feedback to non-expert co-designers as concrete, actionable fixes rather than mere rule citations, while flagging that this single-user finding has not yet been tested in multi-user/collaborative or civic-stakes contexts.

**Cite in supplementary:** A specific methodological precedent worth citing in our supplementary materials: VizCrit's finding that solution-centered feedback significantly boosted self-perceived creativity (CSI) and reduced issues without improving expert-rated creativity is a directly relevant methodological caution for our own evaluation design, it argues for including both a self-report satisfaction/creativity measure AND an independent expert/rubric-based outcome measure (e.g., zoning-compliance expert or planner rating) when we evaluate whether AI-surfaced compliance feedback actually improves community design quality versus merely making participants feel more confident/creative, and for using non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis/Mann-Whitney with Bonferroni) given our likely small between-condition cell sizes in a novel civic tool study.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Solution-centered feedback yielded significantly fewer remaining design issues than textbook-based feedback (p<.05).
- Solution-centered participants self-rated significantly higher creativity, enjoyment, and results-worth-effort on the CSI than textbook-based participants (p<.01), but expert-rated creativity of the actual designs showed NO significant difference across conditions.
- Awareness- and solution-centered groups clicked into the design-principle panel significantly more than textbook-based (p<.01), i.e., situated visual annotation drove more engagement with underlying rationale even when it didn't dictate the fix.
- No significant differences in learning gains (pre/post knowledge test) or degree of design change across conditions.
- Authors explicitly flag a gap between self-perceived and expert-rated creativity as a key nuance: concrete-fix feedback made novices feel more creative and satisfied without objectively improving creative output, though it did improve rule-compliance.

### Spacetime: Enabling Fluid Individual and Collaborative Editing in Virtual Reality
- **Authors:** Haijun Xia, et al.
- **Venue/Year:** ACM Symposium on User Interface Software and Technology (UIST 2018) 2018
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3242587.3242597
- **Relevance:** Xia's foundational multi-user 3D editing work, introducing Containers, Parallel Objects, and Avatar Objects to let multiple users manipulate a shared 3D scene fluidly without stepping on each other's agency. Directly underpins the technical/interaction-design rationale for a multi-user, real-time 3D architectural co-design surface built on Pascal editor.

**Methodology summary:** This is primarily a systems/interaction-design paper; the empirical component is a single-session qualitative expert evaluation, not a controlled experiment. Six professional VR developers (ages 22-41, each with 3+ years VR development experience; 5 of 6 had shipped published VR applications) were recruited. Each did one 80-minute session with the experimenter co-present in VR: (1) 15-20 min guided training on basic manipulation/navigation plus the three novel concepts (Container, Parallel Objects, Avatar Objects), with verbal explanation, participant practice, and experimenter demonstration on request, continuing until the participant could operate unaided; (2) 30-40 min collaborative exercise/exploration in which participant and experimenter jointly populated an empty city map with cars, buildings, and trees, after which additional advanced (not-yet-demonstrated) features were introduced; (3) 15-20 min post-task questionnaire (7-point Likert items, 1=strongly disagree to 7=strongly agree, on usefulness/usability of each concept) plus an open-ended semi-structured interview about how well the system supported individual and collaborative workflows and how participants foresaw it fitting their own production pipelines. Participants were paid $50. System: Unity + Oculus Rift CV1/Touch controllers on desktop PCs (Nvidia GTX 1060), synchronized via Photon Unity Networking SDK, supporting 2-3 co-present users.

**Strengths:**
- Recruited genuine domain experts (professional VR developers, most with shipped VR products) rather than convenience-sample novices, which is well-matched to an interaction-technique/systems contribution where expert judgment on design quality is more informative than naive-user usability scores, a choice CHI reviewers typically credit for 'first-pass' evaluations of novel primitives.
- Mixed-methods design (Likert items + open interview) with a realistic, ecologically meaningful task (populating a city scene collaboratively) rather than an abstract micro-task, giving the quantitative ratings interpretive context via rich supporting quotes.
- Transparent, ungenerous reporting of dissenting/outlier participants (P1's neutral/negative reactions to the projection metaphor, the grabbing-avatar metaphor, and motion sickness; P3/P6's 'overwhelmed' comment) rather than only positive quotes, this kind of candor is something CHI reviewers explicitly reward.
- Clear, reproducible technical apparatus description (Unity, Oculus Rift CV1, Photon Unity Networking, GTX 1060) and a well-structured procedure (training → task → questionnaire/interview) with stated time budgets per phase, making the evaluation auditable even though it's small.
- Explicitly scopes claims to 'intimate collaborative sessions among 2-3 users' and names crowd-scale collaboration as unaddressed future work, rather than overclaiming generalizability.

**Weaknesses:**
- N=6, single session each, no control/baseline condition and no counterbalancing, this is a formative expert walkthrough, not a between/within-subjects experiment; the paper never claims otherwise, but any performance/usability numbers (e.g., Likert breakdowns) cannot support statistical generalization and the paper wisely reports no inferential statistics, only raw counts.
- 'Collaborative' evaluation never actually paired two naive participants together, every collaborative session was participant + one of the researchers acting as co-collaborator. Since the paper's headline contribution is multi-user collaborative editing (Parallel Objects, Avatar Objects), this is a significant construct-validity gap: real peer-peer conflict dynamics, negotiation, and social discomfort (e.g., grabbing another *user's* avatar, not the experimenter's) were never observed between two participants with independent goals/agency.
- Participants were VR development professionals, i.e., highly technically fluent power users predisposed to appreciate novel spatial metaphors (Container-as-projection, Parallel Objects-as-layers), this is close to a best-case sample for adoption enthusiasm and is unlikely to surface the confusion, hesitancy, or metaphor-mismatch that non-expert or lay users would exhibit.
- No inter-rater/thematic-analysis methodology is described for how interview quotes were selected or coded ('themes' appear to be organized post hoc around the three named concepts), raising a risk of selective quotation/confirmation bias, a common CHI-reviewer critique of qualitative HCI systems papers.
- Training continued 'until the participant could interact with the system without help,' an unbounded/uncontrolled learning criterion that makes the subsequent 'easy to learn' Likert ratings somewhat circular (learning was scaffolded to success before the measurement was taken), and the paper doesn't report training duration variance across participants.
- The task (populate a city map with pre-made 3D models) is low-stakes and has no real design constraints, disagreement structure, or external legitimacy pressure, quite different from the adversarial, higher-stakes negotiation dynamic implied by zoning-compliant community co-design against a developer's competing proposal, so ecological validity for that specific use case is limited.
- $50/80-minute compensation and single time point means no data on longitudinal use, fatigue, or whether initial novelty-driven enthusiasm ('I want it now') would persist across repeated real design sessions.

**Sample size / conditions:** N=6 professional VR developers (not novices, not the target community-design population); within-subjects, single condition (no baseline/comparison system, no counterbalancing, no control group); one 80-minute session per participant; collaboration was always participant + one experimenter (a member of the research team), never participant-participant pairs, so "multi-user collaboration" was never tested between two naive users.

**Measures used:** (1) Quantitative: post-task 7-point Likert-scale questionnaire items per concept, e.g. intuitiveness of Container-based selection/manipulation, ease of understanding the "projection of space and time" metaphor, ease of creating/comparing designs and discussing them with collaborators via Parallel Objects, usefulness of Parallel Avatars for indicating where a collaborator should go, overall ease of use and ease of learning of the system. Results reported only as raw response-count breakdowns (e.g. "4/6 strongly agree, 2/6 agree"), no means, SDs, or statistical tests. (2) Qualitative: semi-structured open-ended interview, analyzed by pulling representative verbatim quotes organized thematically under each concept (Containers, Parallel Objects, Avatar Objects) and under a general Usability section; no formal qualitative coding scheme (e.g., no inter-rater reliability, no thematic-analysis methodology described) is reported.

**Supplementary material notes:** Include as a methodological precedent in supplementary materials: Spacetime's n=6-expert, single-session, no-control-group expert-walkthrough design (Likert-count reporting + thematic quotes, no inferential stats) is a legitimate and CHI-precedented way to formatively validate novel interaction primitives before a larger evaluation, useful to cite if reviewers challenge the maturity/rigor of our own early-stage system evaluation. Also worth flagging explicitly in supplementary limitations-of-related-work discussion: Spacetime's own stated scope limit ('intimate collaborative sessions among 2-3 users', crowd-scale explicitly deferred to future work) and its unresolved peer-to-peer evaluation gap (collaboration was always tested against a researcher-confederate, not a second naive participant) are precisely the two axes our own study must NOT reproduce if we want reviewers to see our contribution as advancing beyond Spacetime, i.e., our study should test real peer non-expert dyads/groups (not researcher-as-partner) and ideally beyond 2-3 simultaneous users, to differentiate from this precedent.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the MAIN paper's related work: Spacetime is the direct technical/interaction-design precedent for multi-user real-time 3D editing (its Parallel Objects mechanism is the state-of-the-art answer to exactly the lock-vs-conflict problem a community co-design surface built on Pascal editor must solve when multiple non-expert participants manipulate the same shared building model simultaneously).

- Named, itemized technical/UX limitations list (occlusion, immersion/registration drift, outdoor lighting/weather, battery drain, Adobe Aero click-target confusion, GDPR/personal-data reluctance) reads as a ready-made checklist of AR-in-the-field failure modes.

- **Authors:** (systematic review)
- **Venue/Year:** Sustainable Cities and Society (Elsevier), 2025 2025
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1569843225003930
- **Relevance:** Systematic review of 84 publications (2014-2024) on urban digital twins for built-environment perception and public participation. - Rigorous, transparent PRISMA protocol with a full reported funnel (635 → 210 → 84) and a named quality-appraisal instrument (CASP), which a CHI reviewer would recognize as methodologically sound for a survey/meta-analysis paper.

**Weaknesses:**
- No inter-rater reliability reported for the 16-indicator coding of 84 papers; with a multi-author extraction team (per CRediT statement, several authors handled 'data curation'/'formal analysis') there is no stated dual-coding, adjudication process, or kappa statistic, leaving coding consistency unverified, a clear CHI-reviewer critique for a paper making quantitative claims (frequencies, percentages) from qualitative coding.

### Value-Centered Framing Supports Inclusive Idea Convergence in Participatory Civic Design
- **Authors:** Jeongeon Park, Irene Hou, Risa Sundu, Steven P. Dow
- **Venue/Year:** Collective Intelligence Conference (CI '25), pp. 139 to 149 2025
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3715928.3737470
- **Relevance:** Directly on civic/participatory design convergence and idea consolidation under differing stakeholder values, core to how a community group would converge on a single zoning-compliant design in the proposed tool.

**Methodology summary:** Within-subjects lab/online study, n=24 (12 women, 10 men, 1 non-binary, 1 prefer not to say; 4 with urban design/city planning experience), recruited from the surrounding university community via student group chats and social media. Each participant experienced all 3 conditions in a counterbalanced order: (1) OnlyVoting, vote for favorite of 3 randomly selected ideas, view overall popularity; (2) Value, select one of 5 personal values then a sub-value, vote among 3 ideas tagged to that value, view popularity within-value; (3) Idea+Value, same as Value but preceded by an open-ended idea-contribution step. The interactive prototype (HTML/CSS/JS) used a fixed, pre-tagged idea set drawn from an existing real civic park-planning project in San Diego; it was static (no live vote aggregation reflected back to participants). After each condition, participants completed the 6-item Likert survey plus condition-specific open-ended questions; a brief structured exit interview followed. Analysis: Friedman test per Likert measure to test overall condition effect, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction; a supplementary Friedman + repeated-measures ANOVA checked for order effects (none found, so omitted). Open-ended survey/interview responses were analyzed via deductive coding against categories derived from the paper's research question (inclusion, alignment, understanding, compromise, effort).

**Strengths:**
- Clean, well-specified within-subjects manipulation with proper counterbalancing and an explicit, reported check for order effects (Friedman + repeated-measures ANOVA), which strengthens causal interpretation of the condition effect despite the small sample.
- Appropriate nonparametric statistical pipeline for ordinal Likert data (Friedman omnibus test + Wilcoxon signed-rank pairwise with Bonferroni correction) rather than misapplying parametric tests to 7-point scales.
- Mixed-methods triangulation: quantitative Likert differences are corroborated (and complicated/nuanced) by qualitative interview and open-ended data, e.g., surfacing that the 'preferred' Idea+Value condition also had costs (participants feeling 'trapped' or reduced flexibility to compromise later).
- Grounded, principled derivation of the value taxonomy from an established psychometric instrument (REP scale) rather than ad hoc categories, with a documented (if informal) coding process to map real community ideas onto value domains.
- Honest, specific reporting of disconfirming/complicating participant voices (P10, P12 who didn't care about others' values; P9/P15 who felt 'trapped' by Idea+Value) rather than only reporting the favorable majority pattern.
- Transparent inclusion of full survey instrument and value framework in the appendix, enabling replication or reuse by other researchers (directly reusable for our own supplementary materials).

**Weaknesses:**
- Very small, homogeneous sample (n=24, largely university-affiliated/college-student recruits) explicitly acknowledged by the authors as unrepresentative of a real municipal public, the exact population our CHI tool targets (non-expert 'community groups') is arguably better represented by this paper's population than the general public, but a CHI reviewer would still flag n=24 as thin for 5 separate outcome measures with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise tests, several of which (e.g., Idea+Value vs OnlyVoting alignment, p=.09) sit right at or above conventional significance after correction.
- The convergence task is entirely simulated and low-stakes: participants vote on ideas from someone else's prior civic project, and 'the prototype was static, ideas, selected values, and votes submitted by study participants were not reflected', so there is no real accountability, no real trade-off consequence, and no test of whether stated willingness-to-compromise survives an actual binding outcome (a common criticism of civic-tech HCI studies more broadly).
- Order effect check is reported only as a null result with no test statistics given ('there were no significant results, thus omitted from the results'), a CHI reviewer would want the actual F/χ² and p values rather than a bare assertion, especially given only 24 participants split across 6 possible orderings.
- The paper reduces each participant to a single value/sub-value per round by design, and the authors themselves note this may be a construct-validity limitation (P24 wanted ideas spanning multiple values simultaneously), forcing single-value selection may under-model how real stakeholders reason about zoning trade-offs, which are inherently multi-attribute.
- 'Willingness to Compromise' is operationalized with a single item ('I would still be willing to use the park even if my idea is not implemented') that conflates behavioral intention with attitudinal buy-in, and doesn't distinguish compromise-on-an-idea from compromise-on-a-process, a fairly weak/indirect proxy for the construct the paper's title foregrounds.
- No inter-rater reliability statistics reported for the deductive coding of open-ended/interview data (no Cohen's kappa or similar), making the qualitative synthesis harder to audit for coder bias.
- Ceiling/self-selection risk: participants who volunteer for a civic park-design study (student groups, social media) are plausibly already higher in civic engagement/interest, which could inflate all measures uniformly and may not generalize to reluctant or skeptical stakeholders, a population that matters greatly for a tool meant to build 'legitimacy' against a developer's competing proposal.

**Sample size / conditions:** n=24 participants, within-subjects design, 3 counterbalanced conditions (OnlyVoting baseline vs. two value-scaffolded variants: Value and Idea+Value), single ~1-session study using a static, non-live design probe on a simulated (not real, not consequential) civic decision.

**Measures used:** Six 7-point Likert items administered after each condition: Mental Effort, Confidence, Inclusion, Alignment (with community values/priorities), Community Understanding, and Willingness to Compromise (operationalized as continued willingness to use the park even if one's own idea isn't implemented), full items given in Appendix A.1. Paired with open-ended short-answer questions after each condition and a final structured interview (which condition liked most / felt most involved in / best understood community opinions through). Value taxonomy (Appendix A.2): 5 domains (Achievement/learning, Escape, Social, Physical fitness, Nature) and 21 scale items adapted from the Recreation Experience Preference (REP) scale, selected over Schwartz's basic values as more concrete/accessible to lay users, with domain-tagging of real civic-project ideas done via ChatGPT-assisted then manually revised coding.

**Supplementary material notes:** Appendix A.1 gives the full survey instrument (all 6 Likert items verbatim, plus the condition-specific and shared open-ended questions), directly reusable as a validated instrument template for measuring inclusion/alignment/compromise in our own study. Appendix A.2 gives the full REP-derived value taxonomy (5 domains x 21 scale items) with citation to Manfredo, Driver & Tarrant 1996, useful methodological precedent for how to adapt an abstract values framework (they explicitly rejected Schwartz's Basic Values as 'too abstract... inaccessible to the lay user') into a lay-accessible design vocabulary, which is directly relevant if our tool needs an analogous value/priority elicitation step for zoning tradeoffs (density vs. character vs. affordability etc.).

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the main related-work section as direct empirical precedent that a lightweight value-elicitation step before voting/convergence measurably increases perceived inclusion, community alignment, and willingness to compromise without added cognitive cost, which is the exact mechanism our tool needs for a community group to converge on one zoning-compliant design.

**Cite in supplementary:** The paper's own limitation, a static, non-consequential prototype using pre-existing ideas disconnected from real votes/outcomes, plus a homogeneous university-recruited sample, is a direct methodological precedent for a pitfall we must design around (and disclose) in our own tool's evaluation, since our system claims real decision consequence (zoning-compliant, real building) rather than a simulated probe.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Significant differences across conditions for Inclusion (χ²(2)=16.58, p=.00025), Alignment (χ²(2)=11.19, p=.0037), Community Understanding (χ²(2)=7.11, p=.0285), Willingness to Compromise (χ²(2)=14.91, p=.0006), and Confidence (χ²(2)=7.9, p=.019); Mental Effort showed no significant difference (χ²(2)=4.63, p=.099).
- Inclusion: Idea+Value M=5.04 (SD=1.65) and Value M=4.96 (SD=1.43) both > OnlyVoting M=3.46 (SD=2.02).
- Willingness to Compromise: Value (M=5.83) > Idea+Value (M=5.42) > OnlyVoting (M=4.71); Value significantly higher than Idea+Value (W=4.0, p=.021), the one measure where the two value conditions differed.
- 17 of 24 participants preferred Idea+Value overall, 4 preferred Value, 2 preferred OnlyVoting, 1 had mixed preference, a purely descriptive count, not statistically tested.
- Quote (P22 on OnlyVoting): "This is just normal voting, so it feels more like the United States election... I can't tell why people voted for stuff like pickleball courts."
- Quote (P15 on Idea+Value): "writing down my idea before basically felt like a 'trap' where I had to vote for something that I wrote down beforehand."
- Stated limitation (authors' own words): "Our participants were recruited primarily from the university community, which may not adequately represent the diverse composition of the broader public or other community groups."
- Stated limitation: the prototype was static and used pre-existing ideas from a prior real civic project without reflecting participants' actual votes back into any output, and some participants noted a discrepancy between the ideas shown and the actual park site described.

### VizCrit: Exploring Strategies for Displaying Computational Feedback in a Visual Design Tool
- **Authors:** Mingyi Li, Mengyi Chen, Sarah Luo, Yining Cao, Haijun Xia, Maitraye Das, Steven P. Dow, Jane L. E
- **Venue/Year:** ACM CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), Barcelona 2026
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791579
- **Relevance:** Co-authored with Haijun Xia (another named collaborator); studies actionable, situated computational feedback/annotation in a visual design tool, directly informs how AI-generated zoning/code compliance feedback should be surfaced in the co-design 3D interface.

**Methodology summary:** Between-subjects lab study (N=36 UCSD SONA-recruited novice designers, 60 to 90 min Zoom sessions) comparing three feedback-display strategies in a design-critique tool (VizCrit): (1) textbook-based static text reference, (2) awareness-centered visual annotations (highlight/group/color-code the issue area without prescribing a fix), and (3) solution-centered annotations (red flags + green "solution" arrows + explicit "Suggested Actions" text). All participants revised the same seeded flawed design (one violation per each of 4 visual-design principles: hierarchy, alignment, whitespace, unity) after a pre-test, tool tutorial, and annotation-type tutorial; a post-test, survey, and interview followed. Analysis used non-parametric statistics (Kruskal-Wallis omnibus + Mann-Whitney U pairwise with Bonferroni correction) for the quantitative outcomes, and reflexive thematic analysis for interview data. The feedback-generation pipeline itself (OCR + clustering + rule-based heuristics per principle) was separately validated for accuracy against a benchmark set of 46 designs before being used in the human study.

**Strengths:**
- Rigorous separation of self-perceived vs. expert-rated creativity outcomes surfaced a genuinely interesting and reviewer-pleasing dissociation (subjective creativity boost with no objective creative gain) rather than collapsing everything into one self-report measure, this is a methodological strength CHI reviewers would specifically praise.
- The feedback-generation pipeline was independently benchmarked for detection accuracy (>0.85 across a 46-design test set, with per-principle breakdown) before being used in the human study, which pre-empts the common criticism that an AI-feedback study conflates 'is the feedback display good' with 'is the feedback itself even correct.'
- Rich, triangulated multi-modal measurement: self-report (CSI), independent expert ratings (design issues, creativity), behavioral/interaction logs (clicks, dwell, design-state deltas), and qualitative interviews all pointed at the same three conditions, giving convergent validity.
- Controlled, matched seed design (identical starting artifact, one violation per principle) isolates the effect of feedback *display strategy* cleanly from confounds of differing starting materials.
- Pre-registered-feeling structure with clear operational definitions of each condition (textbook/awareness/solution) backed by an appendix table showing the exact text/visual content shown in every condition, supporting replicability.

**Weaknesses:**
- Small per-cell sample (~12 participants per condition in a between-subjects design) with non-parametric tests limits statistical power for detecting smaller or interaction effects, and increases risk that the significant findings reflect noisy small-sample effects, a CHI reviewer would likely flag this as underpowered for a 3-condition between-subjects design.
- Single session, single task, single seed design: results may not generalize beyond this one revision task and this one set of four visual-design principles (hierarchy/alignment/whitespace/unity), and the authors themselves acknowledge this.
- All participants were UCSD students (SONA pool) skewing young (M=21.4) and heavily novice/female (28F/7M), a narrow, WEIRD, single-institution sample that limits external validity to broader 'novice designer' populations.
- No measurement of design fixation/anchoring, the most theoretically important risk of solution-centered feedback (that showing an explicit 'fix' biases users toward accepting it rather than exploring alternatives) was not directly tested, despite the paper's framing being centrally about actionability tradeoffs.
- Purely immediate/single-session learning-gain measurement (pre/post within the same session) cannot speak to retention or transfer, so the 'no significant learning differences' finding is only a same-day snapshot, not evidence that solution-centered feedback is learning-neutral over time.
- Both design-issue and creativity expert ratings were done by only two raters (in several cases the authors themselves rather than independent blind experts), raising potential rater bias/non-independence concerns that a reviewer might probe (unclear if inter-rater reliability/Cohen's kappa was reported for all rubrics).

**Sample size / conditions:** N=36 novice participants (28F/7M/1 undisclosed, age 18 to 35, M=21.4; 35/36 self-identified as having little-to-no visual design experience), recruited via UCSD SONA, randomly assigned between-subjects to one of three conditions (textbook-based / awareness-centered / solution-centered), n≈12 per condition (not explicitly reported per-cell but implied by even 3-way split of 36). Single design-revision task per participant; no within-subjects/repeated-measures component and no longitudinal follow-up.

**Measures used:** Self-report: Creativity Support Index (CSI, 5-point Likert across Enjoyment, Results-Worth-Effort, etc.) and an AI-usability/annotation-comprehension survey. Design quality: expert-rated Design Issue Count (two raters, 4-point rubric per principle). Creativity: self-perceived (via CSI) vs. expert-rated (two independent raters, 10-point scale). Learning: pre/post 16-point knowledge test split into vocabulary (easy) and application (hard) sub-scores, with learning gain = post−pre. Behavioral: timestamped principle-panel clicks, annotation-view events, and JSON-logged design-state changes via Firebase Firestore; "Degree of Change" coded across canvas element categories (some computed programmatically, some hand-coded by two authors); response-to-annotation coding (accepted suggestion vs. made a relevant but different change). Feedback-quality/accuracy check: three randomly sampled annotations per participant rated 0/0.5/1 for accuracy by two expert authors, plus a separate technical validation of the detection heuristics against 46 benchmark designs (>0.85 accuracy). Qualitative: semi-structured interviews coded via reflexive thematic analysis (inductive+deductive) in Condens.

**Supplementary material notes:** The appendix (A.1 A.4) includes: A.1 images from a design-probe co-design session showing how the three annotation styles were co-developed with participant input on visual iterations; A.2 a full table of text-based explanations mirrored across all annotation conditions per principle; A.3 a table of the static textbook reference content (definitions/examples/recommended actions) shown in all conditions; A.4 system implementation details, WebSocket frontend/backend, OpenCV OCR for text extraction, HarfBuzz for line-height measurement, K-means clustering for hierarchy analysis, coordinate-based grouping for alignment, pairwise spacing analysis for whitespace, property-variance counting for unity, and Firebase Firestore for logging. A separate technical evaluation section validates heuristic accuracy on 20 public + 26 custom edge-case designs (>0.85 overall, per-principle breakdown given) and verifies annotation generation across 152 cases via author visual inspection.

**Cite in main paper:** Cite in the MAIN paper: VizCrit provides the closest empirical precedent for the central open design question in the co-design tool, whether AI-generated compliance/design feedback should be shown as awareness-only annotations (flagging an issue) or solution-centered annotations (flagging + suggesting a fix), and its finding that solution-centered feedback boosts perceived creativity/productivity without objective creative cost or learning cost directly justifies choosing a more prescriptive, actionable feedback style for surfacing zoning/code violations in the 3D interface.

**Cite in supplementary:** The specific methodological precedent worth recording in OUR supplementary materials is VizCrit's three-tier feedback-actionability taxonomy (textbook-based / awareness-centered / solution-centered) plus its explicit design of a 0/0.5/1 accuracy-verification pass on a random subsample of annotations per participant, this is a directly reusable protocol for validating AI-generated zoning/code-compliance annotations in our tool, and their unresolved limitation (not measuring design fixation/anchoring from solution-centered suggestions) is exactly the risk our system must guard against when suggesting compliant fixes to a community group's design and should be flagged as a known open problem we inherit.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "Solution-centered feedback led to fewer design issues and higher self-perceived creativity compared with textbook-based feedback," though expert evaluations showed no meaningful creativity differences, a striking dissociation between subjective and expert-rated creative quality.
- Both awareness-centered and solution-centered conditions produced significantly more engagement with the design-principles panel than textbook-based feedback (p<.01), suggesting visual/spatial situating of feedback (vs. text-only reference material) drives engagement independent of actionability level.
- No significant differences in learning gains (pre/post knowledge test) across any of the three feedback conditions, actionable, solution-centered feedback did not measurably hurt or help learning in this single-session study.
- Authors explicitly flag that they did not directly measure design fixation/anchoring effects from solution-centered suggestions, despite that being the most theoretically important risk of showing users a 'fix' rather than just an 'issue'.
- Feedback accuracy was independently verified: heuristic detection algorithms tested on 46 designs (20 public + 26 custom edge cases) achieved >0.85 overall accuracy, with per-principle breakdown (Hierarchy 0.95, Alignment 0.79, Whitespace 0.84, Unity 0.70), i.e., they validated the feedback-generation pipeline itself before running the human study.

### Self-Reflective Crowds: Surfacing Wisdom through Emergent Scaffolding
- **Authors:** Ruo Ning (Nancy) Qiu, Annapurna Vadaparty, Suma Vintha, Steven P. Dow
- **Venue/Year:** Collective Intelligence Conference (CI '25), pp. 169 to 187 2025
- **URL:** https://spdow.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Steven_Dow_CV-Aug2025.pdf
- **Relevance:** Explores emergent scaffolding for surfacing collective wisdom in crowds, relevant to how a multi-user civic design tool can scaffold non-expert groups toward higher-quality collective judgments without top-down facilitation.

**Methodology summary:** The paper studies "emergent scaffolding", a workflow where crowd workers themselves generate the hints, rubrics, and graded examples that train subsequent workers, rather than requesters or experts pre-authoring guidance. The system operationalizes an iterative four-step "Train-Try-Reflect-Synthesize" (TTRS) pattern across three sequential batches of crowd workers doing a creative task (illustrating scientific papers): Batch 1 does the task with generic/minimal training and writes reflective hints; two researchers + an LLM (GPT-4o-mini) open-code those hints into themes and derive a five-item rubric; Batch 2 rates/grades prior examples against the rubric; the rubric + graded examples are synthesized (again via researcher + LLM collaboration, testing 5 input-quality conditions: Good/Decent/Bad-with-code/Bad-no-code/No-data) into final "emergent scaffolding" materials; Batch 3 receives these as training. The core evaluation is a separate between-subjects experiment (not just the 3-batch pipeline) with three conditions, InstructionsOnly, GenericExample, EmergentScaffolding, each subject randomly assigned one of six papers (3 easy/3 hard), completing a ~30-minute asynchronous Qualtrics task, one condition per participant. Task quality was scored by six expert raters on the crowd-derived five-item rubric, with inter-rater calibration (Cohen's kappa >0.7, final ICC 0.8-0.9, Cronbach's alpha .753). Analysis used one-way ANOVAs (condition effect on quality; also paper topic and paper difficulty as separate factors) with Tukey's HSD post-hoc contrasts and Cohen's d effect sizes, plus additional ANOVAs testing demographic covariates (English proficiency, education level, design experience, scientific literacy) and a two-way ANOVA (condition x collapsed design-experience) for an interaction check. Self-efficacy/perception was measured via 4 Likert items (confidence completing task, confidence meeting expectations, instruction clarity, uncertainty reduction) plus a 10-point self-assessed-quality slider, supplemented by open-ended qualitative feedback analyzed thematically/anecdotally (quoted, not systematically coded with reliability stats reported in the visible sections).

**Strengths:**
- Strong, transparent quantitative rigor for a crowd-work HCI study: pre-registered-style multi-factor ANOVA design controlling for paper difficulty/topic/duration, Tukey's HSD post-hocs with CIs and Cohen's d effect sizes reported throughout (not just p-values), exactly the reporting standard CHI reviewers now expect.
- Blind expert rating with documented inter-rater reliability (kappa>0.7, ICC 0.8-0.9, rubric Cronbach's alpha .753) guards against the common crowd-quality-eval weakness of unvalidated single-rater scoring.

### DesignWeaver: Dimensional Scaffolding for Text-to-Image Product Design
- **Authors:** Sirui Tao, Ivan Liang, Cindy Peng, Zhiqing Wang, Srishti Palani, Steven P. Dow
- **Venue/Year:** ACM CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '25) 2025
- **URL:** https://spdow.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Steven_Dow_CV-Aug2025.pdf
- **Relevance:** Shows how scaffolding generative-AI interaction with structured design dimensions helps novices produce more diverse, domain-appropriate outputs, a direct analogue for scaffolding non-expert community members' voice/AI-assisted 3D building design choices.

**Methodology summary:** Two-part mixed-methods study. (1) Formative study: 30-60 min semi-structured interviews with 12 experienced product/architecture/industrial/furniture/landscape designers (2-21 yrs experience, recruited via LinkedIn/Reddit/Discord), analyzed via open coding to identify recurring themes about how experts use visual references (not just written descriptions) to negotiate design spaces with less-expert clients, this motivated the dimensional-scaffolding interface. (2) Evaluation study: between-subjects controlled experiment, 52 novices (19-31 yrs, 36 female, 29 with no design background, 41 who had never used a text-to-image model) randomized to DesignWeaver (n=27, a dimension palette surfaced from generated images for quick selection into prompts) vs. a ChatGPT-like text-only baseline (n=25 after 1 exclusion for copy-pasting the brief verbatim as a prompt). All participants did the same 20-minute task, design a dining chair from a brief, client persona, sketches, and mood board, after a 5-min scenario review and 5-min tool tutorial, followed by a 20-30 min post-survey and semi-structured interview. Analysis pipeline: custom NLP (stopword filtering, POS tagging, TF-IDF, Word2Vec) for prompt vocabulary/length/unique design-term counts; CLIP (ViT-B/32) embedding similarity and Levenshtein edit distance for measuring image and prompt diversity/iteration distance; blind expert ratings (n=6 designers with 2-5 yrs experience) scoring outputs 1-7 on novelty and brief-alignment; Likert (1-7) UX survey items on ease of idea-to-prompt translation, exploration, refinement, and term-learning; thematic analysis of interview transcripts. Group differences tested with non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests (given non-normal distributions), visualized via violin plots, alpha=.05.

**Strengths:**
- Genuine mixed-methods triangulation: a needfinding qualitative study with real domain experts grounds the design of the scaffolding mechanism before the quantitative evaluation, which CHI reviewers reward as good 'research through design' practice.
- Multi-modal outcome measurement (NLP text metrics + CLIP embedding similarity + Levenshtein edit distance + blind expert ratings + self-report Likert + interviews) triangulates 'diversity/quality of output' rather than relying on a single self-report proxy, which is unusually rigorous for a novice-creativity-support-tool study.
- Uses a real between-subjects control condition (a plausible ChatGPT-like baseline) rather than only a within-subjects or no-baseline design, enabling causal claims about the scaffolding intervention itself.
- Appropriately uses non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U) given non-normal distributions rather than defaulting to t-tests, and reports effect sizes/visualizations (violin plots) rather than just p-values.
- Honest reporting of a null/counter-intuitive result (no satisfaction difference despite better objective output) and of a paradoxical usability complaint (over-tagging), rather than only reporting confirmatory findings, this kind of negative-finding transparency is something CHI reviewers specifically praise.
- Blind third-party expert raters (distinct from the interviewed formative-study experts) reduce self-report bias in judging design quality/novelty.

**Weaknesses:**
- Single fixed task domain and object (dining chair only) with a 20-minute time box, a CHI reviewer would flag that this narrow, short, single-artifact task limits generalizability to other product domains, more complex objects, or longer real-world design timelines.
- Between-subjects n is modest (27 vs 25 after exclusion) for a design task with likely high individual variance in creativity/prior exposure to design vocabulary; no power analysis reported to justify sample size, a common CHI methodological ask.
- Single ad hoc exclusion (1 baseline participant removed for 'outlier' copy-paste behavior) is a post hoc judgment call that could bias baseline-condition results upward or downward depending on how outlier prompts were being weighted elsewhere; the exclusion criterion is not clearly pre-registered.
- Blind expert panel is only n=6 raters, and inter-rater reliability (e.g., ICC, Krippendorff's alpha) for the 1-7 novelty/alignment ratings is not reported in the summarized methodology, which reviewers would want to see to trust that 'more diverse/novel' finding.
- Demographic skew (mostly college-aged 19-31, majority female, many recruited in-person likely from a university population) limits generalizability to a broader or more heterogeneous 'novice community member' population, directly relevant since our own project targets non-expert *community* stakeholders, not university-recruited novices.
- The formative study's 12 experts were interviewed but not directly observed in live co-design sessions with clients, so the 'visual reference over verbal description' claim rests on self-reported recollection rather than in situ behavioral observation.
- The central unresolved tension the paper surfaces (scaffolding raises quality but also raises expectations, producing no net satisfaction gain) is acknowledged but not resolved with a follow-up manipulation (e.g., expectation-setting or explicit model-capability disclosure), leaving an open methodological gap rather than a tested fix.

**Sample size / conditions:** Formative study: n=12 expert designers, interview-only, no experimental conditions. Evaluation study: n=52 novices total (27 DesignWeaver condition, 25 baseline condition after excluding 1 outlier), single between-subjects factor (DesignWeaver vs. text-only ChatGPT-like baseline), single fixed 20-minute chair-design task, mixed remote (5) and in-person (47) administration.

**Measures used:** Prompt length (word count) and unique/domain-specific design-term counts via custom NLP pipeline (stopwords, POS tagging, TF-IDF, Word2Vec); output diversity via CLIP (ViT-B/32) embedding similarity across generated images and prompts; iteration/edit distance via Levenshtein distance between successive prompts; blind expert panel ratings (n=6 designers) of novelty and brief-alignment on 1-7 scales; number of prompts/iterations issued and time per iteration; post-task Likert-scale (1-7) self-report survey on ease of translating ideas to prompts, exploring the design space, refining concepts, and learning design vocabulary; semi-structured post-task interviews coded thematically. Between-condition comparisons used Mann-Whitney U tests.

**Supplementary material notes:** Two details from DesignWeaver belong in our supplementary materials as methodological precedent. First, their triangulated evaluation battery for 'did scaffolding actually help novices produce better/more diverse outputs' (prompt-vocabulary NLP analysis + CLIP embedding diversity + blind expert 1-7 novelty/alignment ratings + Likert self-report + interviews) is a strong template to cite/adapt when we design our own measures for whether voice/AI scaffolding helps community members produce more diverse, zoning-appropriate 3D design options, we should note in supplementary methods that we considered/adapted this multi-measure approach rather than relying on self-report alone. Second, their most important limitation is directly cautionary for us: scaffolding increased objective output quality/diversity but did NOT increase participant satisfaction, because richer, more capable-feeling prompting raised expectations beyond what the underlying generative model (or, in our case, the zoning/structural feasibility engine) could actually deliver, and a subset of users found the structured dimension palette 'over-constricting.' We should flag this in our supplementary limitations/design-rationale section as a known risk to watch for: if our scaffolded voice/AI interface makes non-expert users feel like sophisticated co-designers, we may need to explicitly manage expectations about what is buildable/zoning-compliant, and we should pilot-test whether our scaffolding (e.g., zoning constraint chips, material/typology palettes) similarly risks feeling over-constricting to some community participants.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the main paper's related-work section as direct empirical precedent that structuring/scaffolding generative-AI prompting around explicit, selectable design dimensions (rather than free-text prompting) measurably helps novices produce more diverse, domain-appropriate design outputs, which is the core mechanism our system borrows for scaffolding non-expert community members' voice/AI-assisted 3D building decisions.

**Cite in supplementary:** Not required as a separate supplementary citation, but see supplementary_material_notes for a methodological precedent worth importing.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- DesignWeaver participants wrote significantly longer prompts with more domain-specific vocabulary and produced more diverse/novel designs (per CLIP diversity metrics and blind expert novelty ratings) than baseline.
- Despite richer prompts and more novel outputs, DesignWeaver participants reported no higher satisfaction with final images than baseline (p=0.579), the paper attributes this to 'heightened expectations' outstripping what current text-to-image models can actually render.
- DesignWeaver participants issued fewer total prompts/iterations than baseline while still achieving more diverse outcomes, contradicting an assumed 'more iteration = better' relationship.

### Cueing the Crowd: LLM-Driven Conversational Cues Across Different Meeting Modalities
- **Authors:** Jude Abishek Rayan, Shaokang Jiang, Nishant Balaji, Jinmao Wang, Ian Gross, Cole Biehle, Steven P. Dow
- **Venue/Year:** Collective Intelligence Conference (CI '25) 2025
- **URL:** https://spdow.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Steven_Dow_CV-Aug2025.pdf
- **Relevance:** Studies LLM-generated conversational cues to facilitate group brainstorming across meeting modalities, directly applicable to voice-first AI facilitation during synchronous multi-user 3D design sessions.

**Methodology summary:** A 2x2 between-subjects lab experiment testing a system called CueTip, which uses GPT-4o-mini to generate real-time conversational "cues" (text prompts) delivered roughly once per minute during a live brainstorming conversation. The two factors were Access-to-Cue (Cue vs. NoCue) and Modality (in-person vs. virtual, i.e., same room vs. separate rooms on a video call), giving four conditions: Cue-Virtual, Cue-InPerson, NoCue-Virtual, NoCue-InPerson. 172 participants were randomly assigned in 86 dyads (2 sessions dropped for audio echo, 2 for technical issues). Each dyad did a warm-up "familiarity building" task then a 20-minute "alternative uses" ideation task about recyclable beach-cleanup trash, jotting ideas as individual bullet-point notes, followed by a Qualtrics think-aloud post-survey. All sessions were audio/video recorded and transcribed (Assembly AI); notes, transcripts, and generated cues were logged to Firebase.

Outcome measures: (1) Number of Ideas (deduplicated by researchers from notes+transcript); (2) Topical Diversity of Generated Ideas, ideas pooled across all groups, embedded and clustered with BERTopic/HDBSCAN (min cluster size 15), diversity scored via Shannon entropy; (3) Topic Diversity of Conversation, transcript utterances mapped to Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB) taxonomy topics (698 categories, 3 hierarchy levels), diversity = count of unique Level-1 topics per group; (4) Noticing Rate of Cues, two coders manually marked Y/N per cue based on whether participants verbally referenced/read it aloud, with double-coding of 41 sessions to check intercoder agreement before splitting the remainder between single coders; (5) post-survey Likert items on distraction, timeliness, task difficulty/success/control, analyzed in JMP Pro.

Analysis: REML (restricted maximum likelihood) mixed models with Access-to-Cue x Modality (and interaction) as fixed effects and Prior Design Experience / Recycling Knowledge (each collapsed to a 3-level ordinal factor: 0/1/2 participants experienced) as random effects, run separately for each of the three productivity DVs, followed by Tukey HSD post-hoc tests. Non-parametric Wilcoxon and Spearman rank-correlation tests were used for the noticing-rate and attention-vs-outcome analyses. A supplementary appendix analysis used cosine similarity of idea/cue embeddings to average group embeddings (with a Wilcoxon test) to check for AI-induced homogenization across independent groups, and to validate a proposed Wikipedia-grounded fix to cue repetition.

**Strengths:**
- Clean, well-powered 2x2 factorial RCT with random assignment to 4 conditions and N=172 (86 dyads), much larger than typical HCI lab studies of this kind, giving real statistical power for the REML models.
- Triangulates multiple, largely independent measures of the same construct (topic diversity of ideas via BERTopic/entropy AND topic diversity of raw conversation via an external, validated commercial taxonomy, IAB), which converge on the same conclusion and reduces the risk that the finding is an artifact of one measurement choice.
- Directly controls a key confound: cue dosage/timing was fixed at ~1/minute in all Cue conditions specifically 'to maintain consistency with the number of cues across conditions,' isolating the effect of cue content/presence from cue frequency.
- Includes a genuine null result reported honestly (no effect on number of ideas) rather than only reporting the positive diversity finding, and the discussion section (6.1) engages seriously with why the null result occurred rather than downplaying it.
- Goes beyond the RCT to interrogate an unintended side effect the authors themselves surfaced (cue repetition/homogenization across independent groups) and empirically tests a candidate fix (Wikipedia-grounded pipeline) with its own before/after statistical comparison, a self-critical, iterative move reviewers tend to reward.
- Mixed-methods integration: qualitative think-aloud themes (5.3.1-5.3.4) are used to explain and contextualize the quantitative effects (e.g., why cues help attention/momentum but not raw quantity), rather than being a bolted-on afterthought.
- Transparent, detailed appendix with exact task prompts, survey instruments, and the homogenization-analysis methodology, enabling replication.

**Weaknesses:**
- Sample is a convenience sample skewed toward psychology/cognitive science undergraduates recruited mostly for course credit, the authors themselves flag this as a likely selection bias limiting generalizability to the kind of heterogeneous, non-expert community members a civic co-design tool would need to serve.
- Dyads only (pairs), no test of larger groups, so findings about attention/social dynamics may not transfer to multi-person (3+) synchronous sessions, which is the actual target configuration for a community co-design tool.
- Fixed one-cue-per-minute schedule, chosen for experimental control, is explicitly acknowledged by the authors as possibly suboptimal, participants in the qualitative data asked for on-demand or adaptively-timed cues rather than a metronome; the null result on idea quantity may be partly an artifact of this rigid, non-adaptive delivery policy rather than a fundamental property of cueing.
- Noticing-rate coding relied on manual, subjective judgment of whether a cue was 'explicitly mentioned or read aloud'; only 41 of ~84 valid sessions were double-coded for reliability, and no quantitative intercoder-agreement statistic (e.g., Cohen's kappa) is reported, the paper simply asserts 'strong intercoder agreement' without a number, which a CHI reviewer would likely flag.
- No expert/human ratings of idea novelty, creativity, or feasibility (explicitly stated as skipped 'due to the large number of ideas'), topical diversity via clustering entropy is a proxy for creative breadth, not creative quality, so the headline claim ('increase topical diversity') is narrower than a claim about better ideas.
- Task domain is a single fixed brainstorming prompt (recyclable beach-cleanup trash, an alternative-uses task), a low-stakes, generic creativity task very different from a real, consequential, zoning-constrained architectural design task with tradeoffs and stakeholders, limiting ecological validity for a civic co-design use case.
- Lab-only setting (quiet rooms, controlled AV setup) with no test in noisy/real-world environments, which the authors flag as a limitation for future ASR/notification robustness.
- The homogenization finding (182 of 578 cues repeated verbatim across independent dyads) is a fairly serious threat to using LLM facilitation in a civic/community context (risk of steering many independent community groups toward the same ideas, undermining claims of authentic diverse community input), and the proposed fix (Wikipedia-grounded pipeline) was only validated via an offline embedding-similarity comparison, not re-tested with live human groups, so it remains an untested mitigation.
- Statistical reporting mixes REML F-tests, Tukey HSD, Wilcoxon, and Spearman correlations across different sub-analyses without correction for multiple comparisons across the several correlational tests reported in 5.2, which a stats-focused CHI reviewer might question.

**Sample size / conditions:** N=172 participants in 86 dyads, randomly assigned to a 2x2 between-subjects design (Access-to-Cue: Cue/NoCue x Modality: In-Person/Virtual), ~43 dyads per cell (exact per-cell n not reported in main text, only aggregate N and dyad count). Gender balanced across conditions (M=42, F=127, NB=3); age 18-37 (median 21). Compensation: course credit or $20 for a 75-minute session. Session duration: ~20 min ideation task within a longer protocol. Two sessions excluded for audio echo, two for technical failure; 13 participants excluded from covariate-adjusted analyses for missing pre-survey data.

**Measures used:** Number of ideas generated (deduplicated by hand); Topical diversity of generated ideas (BERTopic + HDBSCAN clustering, Shannon entropy); Topic diversity of live conversation (utterances mapped to IAB 698-topic taxonomy, unique Level-1 topic count); Noticing rate of cues (manual transcript coding, Y/N, with a double-coded reliability subset of 41 sessions); post-study Qualtrics survey (Likert items on task difficulty/success/control/distraction/timeliness) plus open-ended think-aloud responses analyzed qualitatively; appendix homogenization measure (cosine similarity of each idea/cue embedding to the average embedding within its condition).

**Supplementary material notes:** Two methodological precedents worth importing into our supplementary materials: (1) their triangulated topic-diversity measure, pairing a BERTopic/HDBSCAN + Shannon-entropy analysis of generated artifacts with an independent taxonomy-based diversity measure of the raw conversation transcript (they used the IAB taxonomy; we could substitute a zoning/design-element taxonomy), is a reusable template for how we'd operationalize 'diversity of design ideas generated' in our own voice-facilitated 3D co-design sessions. (2) Their explicitly reported limitation and follow-up analysis on AI-cue homogenization across independent groups (182/578 cues repeated verbatim across different dyads, addressed via a Wikipedia-grounded diversification pipeline) is a direct methodological warning we should cite as precedent in a limitations/pre-registration section: if we use LLM facilitation across many independent community design groups, we must pre-plan a check for whether the AI nudges different community groups toward convergent, homogenized design proposals, which would undermine the paper's core legitimacy claim that community groups produce authentically distinct, community-specific alternatives.

**Cite in main paper:** Cite it in the main related-work section as the closest existing empirical evidence that LLM-driven real-time conversational cues measurably increase topical diversity of group-generated ideas without reducing idea quantity across both virtual and in-person modalities, directly supporting (and giving a testable-effects vocabulary for) our claim that voice-first AI facilitation can improve, not merely coexist with, synchronous multi-user design conversation.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Abstract: "In a 2x2 study, N=172 participants in dyads completed a brainstorming task where they received cues or not during either a virtual or in-person discussion. Cued participants' ideas and discussions were more topically diverse than Non-Cued participants. In-person groups noticed slightly more cues on average than virtual groups."
- REML on topic diversity of ideas: F(1,67.03)=6.51, p=0.013, Cue μ=2.3(SD.3) vs NoCue μ=2.1(SD.4); Tukey HSD p≈0.012.
- REML on conversation topic diversity: F(1,50.76)=6.25, p=0.016, Cue μ=9.6(SD2.2) vs NoCue μ=8.0(SD2.5).

- Noticing-rate/attentiveness to cues was hand-coded from transcripts+recordings by two researchers marking Y/N per cue-mention, with double-coding of 41 sessions to establish agreement before single-coder pass on the remainder ('due to a strong intercoder agreement').

### Prototyping Dynamics: Sharing Multiple Designs Improves Exploration, Group Rapport, and Results
- **Authors:** Steven P. Dow, Julie Fortuna, Dan Schwartz, Beth Altringer, Daniel L. Schwartz, Scott R. Klemmer
- **Venue/Year:** ACM CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '11) 2011
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1978942.1979359
- **Relevance:** Foundational study (Dow's Stanford HCI Lab era) showing that sharing multiple parallel design alternatives, versus a single design, improves divergence, consensus-building, and group rapport; grounds the paper's argument for generating and comparing multiple AI-assisted design alternatives with community groups rather than critiquing one developer proposal.

**Methodology summary:** 

**Strengths:**
- Three-way condition design (Multiple / Best / One) rather than a simple two-condition A/B, this is the standout methodological strength: it factorially separates the confound between merely *producing* multiple alternatives and *sharing* multiple alternatives with a collaborator, which a naive single-vs-multiple study would not disentangle.
- Uses a real, live outcome measure (actual Google AdWords click-through on a 12-day campaign for a real non-profit) rather than only self-report or lab-rated quality, strong ecological validity, unusual for a lab creativity study, and the paper is candid that this measure diverges from expert/crowd ratings (R2=0.018), which strengthens rather than weakens the paper's credibility.
- Converging multi-method measurement: objective behavioral coding (speech turns from video), independent blind judge ratings from three different populations (client/professional/crowd), validated psychometric instruments (Subjective Value Inventory, Inclusion of Other in the Self scale) for the social/rapport construct rather than an ad hoc questionnaire, and content-coded feature 'borrowing' as a behavioral trace of idea integration, the rapport finding is corroborated by an independent, more objective measure (turn-taking), reducing common-method-bias concerns.
- Controls for confounds: equal total time budget across conditions (so 'multiple' conditions aren't just getting more practice time), a deliberately unfamiliar design tool to equalize prior tool expertise, stratified randomization balancing gender and design experience across conditions, and blind/independent raters for quality and similarity judgments.
- Reports a full complement of null/contrasting results honestly (e.g., no condition difference in total time spent, total speaking time, or number of speaker turns; quality ratings vs. click-through uncorrelated; experience helps ratings but not campaign clicks) rather than only significant confirmatory findings, increases credibility and gives a textured picture of where the effect does and doesn't show up.
- Grounds quantitative results in qualitative interview excerpts (e.g., participants describing 'mash-ups' vs. 'sticking' with an idea), providing convergent mixed-methods support and mechanism-level interpretation rather than pure statistics.
- Transparent, reusable methodological appendix on the practical pitfalls of using live ad-network analytics as an experimental dependent variable (uneven ad rotation, finite market capacity/impressions ceiling, non-substitutable outcome metrics), genuinely useful guidance for other researchers borrowing this paradigm.

**Weaknesses:**
- Small, underpowered cells for some analyses: only 84 participants / 42 pairs across 3 conditions (~14 pairs/condition), and the turn-taking analysis explicitly reports only 39-40 degrees of freedom (df=39), i.e., a subset of pairs with usable video, further shrinking an already modest per-condition n, effect sizes for interaction terms (e.g., condition x experience) are likely unstable.
- Convenience/self-selected sample recruited via flyers, online ads, and email lists (not a probability sample); mean age 26.5 and ~75% students, a young, non-representative, US Bay-Area-adjacent population whose collaboration norms and design literacy may not generalize to other populations (a real concern for the citing paper's community/non-expert context).
- Task and stakes are artificial and low: a single 30-minute session designing a banner ad for a client the participants have no ongoing relationship with, with strangers paired for the first time (icebreaker notwithstanding), the paper's own discussion section flags this, questioning whether results generalize to longitudinal, cross-functional, power-asymmetric real teams, which is exactly the CHI reviewer critique to expect.
- The live-AdWords click-through metric, while ecologically valid, is a noisy, small-magnitude outcome (239 total clicks across 274,539 impressions, ~$1.51/click) and is subject to platform-level confounds (differential ad rotation favoring early winners) that the authors admit they had to actively manage/monitor rather than fully control, the resulting chi-squared campaign-level tests are also non-independent-observation tests applied to what are effectively impression-level Bernoulli trials clustered by ad, a statistical modeling simplification a stats-savvy reviewer might flag.
- Mechanism is confounded and explicitly left unresolved: the paper cannot cleanly separate whether benefits come from (a) the psychological effect of producing multiple parallel concepts (reduced fixation/sunk cost) vs. (b) the social/exposure effect of viewing a partner's multiple concepts, the Share Best condition partially addresses this but the authors themselves flag it as needing follow-up work with a design that further factors these apart (e.g., providing pre-made examples instead of partner-generated ones).
- Rapport measure is self-report survey administered by the same in-person proctor immediately around a video-recorded discussion, raising mild social-desirability/demand-characteristic risk, and the "rapport" construct blends negotiation-style SVI items with a single graphic IOS item, a somewhat ad hoc composite rather than a single validated multi-item rapport scale.
- Multiple comparisons across many DVs (performance, ratings by 3 rater types, exploration, rapport, turns, feature-borrowing, consensus) without an explicit correction (e.g., no Bonferroni/FDR mentioned), plausible in a 2011 CHI paper but a modern reviewer would likely ask about family-wise error given the sheer number of significance tests reported.

**Sample size / conditions:** 84 participants run as 42 dyads (strangers paired on arrival). Between-subjects design, 3 conditions via stratified randomization balancing gender (41 female) and graphic-design knowledge across pairs: (1) Share Multiple, create 3 ads, share all 3; (2) Share Best, create 3 ads, share only 1 (self-selected "best"); (3) Share One, spend the entire design budget on and share just 1 ad. Multiple vs. Best isolates the effect of sharing multiple designs holding "producing multiple" constant; Best vs. One isolates the effect of producing multiple. All conditions got equal total time (30 min individual design, same critique/discussion time). Participants: mean age 26.5, ~75% students; 36/84 classified "experienced" via a 10-item true/false graphic-design quiz (>=8 correct), rest "novice." Task: real Web banner ad for an actual non-profit client (FaceAIDS.org), built in an unfamiliar tool (flashimageeditor.com) to neutralize prior tool expertise. Full procedure: consent -> icebreakers (block tower, Operation, animal-naming, 3 min each) -> tool training -> practice ad -> design-brief video -> individual design (fresh design forced every 10 min in Multiple/Best) -> dyadic group discussion (5 min critique + 5 min consensus talk, video-recorded) -> final individual design -> open-ended group interview -> debrief. Paid $20 for 120 minutes.

**Measures used:** Converging objective, judged, and process/social measures. (1) Real ad performance: final ads actually ran on Google AdWords for a 12-day live campaign; click-through rate and Google Analytics (time on site, pages visited). (2) Expert/crowd quality ratings: 30 independent, condition-blind judges (3 FaceAIDS clients, 6 ad professionals, 21 Mechanical Turk workers) rated each ad 1-7 on predicted performance. (3) Individual exploration/divergence: 10 MTurk raters judged pairwise similarity (1-7) across each participant's own set of preliminary designs. (4) Group rapport: 5-item survey administered before and after the critique discussion, 4 items from the Subjective Value Inventory (impression, satisfaction, trust, foundation for future interaction) plus the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) pictorial scale; rapport *shift* (post minus pre) was the dependent variable. (5) Conversational dynamics: video-coded speech turns/duration per partner (turns per minute, total speaking time, high/low talker ratio). (6) Design feature "cross-pollination": binary coding of whether a partner's final ad borrowed a feature (word phrases, background color, images, layout, styles/fonts) that was present in the *partner's* shared design but absent from the participant's own prior designs. (7) Group consensus: independent MTurk raters scored partner-ad similarity before vs. after the discussion; increase = consensus. Analyses: chi-squared tests for click-through/campaign counts, ANOVAs (condition x experience) with Tukey post-hoc for ratings/similarity/rapport/turn-taking, paired t-tests for pre/post rapport and similarity shifts.

**Supplementary material notes:** This PDF (hci.stanford.edu/publications/2011/PrototypingDynamics/PrototypingDynamics-CHI2011.pdf) already contains the full appendices inline with the main text: Appendix A is the 10-item true/false graphic-design-knowledge assessment used to classify participants as experienced/novice, and Appendix B is the full advertising design brief given to participants (client goals, target audience, theme/aesthetic requirements, rules). There is no separate/additional supplementary materials file beyond what's bundled in this single 10-page PDF, no separate appendix PDF, dataset, or code repository was found. A companion slide deck exists (PrototypingDynamics-CHI2011Slides.pdf, same directory) but was not needed since the full paper text with appendices was already retrieved.

**Cite in main paper:** Cite in the main related-work section as the foundational empirical grounding for the paper's central design claim: it is the direct evidential source showing that having groups create and share multiple parallel design alternatives (vs. one) causally improves exploration, consensus-building, and interpersonal rapport, the exact mechanism the proposed tool operationalizes for community co-design vs. single-developer-proposal critique.

**Cite in supplementary:** Yes, two details are worth carrying into supplementary methodology material as precedent: (1) the three-condition factorial logic (Share Multiple vs. Share Best vs. Share One) as a template for how our own study could separate "AI generates multiple alternatives" from "community sees multiple alternatives" if we run a controlled comparison; and (2) their validated rapport instrument (Subjective Value Inventory relationship items + Inclusion of Other in the Self scale, measured pre/post as a *shift* score) as a reusable, off-the-shelf measure for community group rapport/cohesion outcomes, rather than inventing an ad hoc rapport questionnaire.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Share Multiple ads got a significantly higher live click-through rate (χ2=4.72, p<.05): 106 clicks/98,867 impressions vs. 57/77,558 (Share Best) and 76/98,038 (Share One).
- Share Multiple ads were rated significantly higher by all three judge types (clients, ad pros, Turkers) (F(2,2519)=5.075, p<.05); Share Best vs Share One did not differ.
- Individual exploration: Share Multiple ads were most divergent, then Share Best, then Share One (F(2,3640)=82.07, p<.05), a clean dose-response ordering across all three conditions.
- Group rapport rose only in Share Multiple (+0.89) and fell in Share Best (-1.75) and Share One (-2.11); condition effect F(2,83)=4.147, p<.05. Across all conditions there was a small but significant overall decline in rapport post-critique, i.e., critique itself is somewhat corrosive to rapport and Share Multiple is what reverses/buffers that.
- Share Multiple pairs took significantly more conversational turns per minute (12.1 vs 9.1 vs 8.6, F(2,39)=3.506, p<.05) but NOT more total turns or total speaking time, the effect is about exchange frequency/rhythm, not volume.
- Share Multiple pairs borrowed significantly more design features from partners (32 vs 18 vs 19 features, χ2=4.05, p<.05) and showed the largest increase in partner-ad similarity after discussion (0.91 vs 0.55 vs 0.52), interpreted as consensus, explicitly distinguished from groupthink because it followed broad exploration rather than replacing it.
- Experience moderated results in opposite directions on different measures: experienced participants' click-through rates benefited MORE from Share Multiple (interaction significant), while novices' subjective quality RATINGS benefited more from Share Multiple than experienced participants' ratings did, the two outcome measures diverge.
- Rated quality and actual click-through performance were uncorrelated (R2=0.018, p>.05); the 3rd-highest-performing ad by clicks was the 2nd-lowest rated ad, explicitly flagged by the authors as a validity point: which metric counts as 'quality' is itself ambiguous.
- The paper's own discussion section separates two candidate mechanisms (producing multiple vs. being exposed to a partner's multiple) and states future work is needed to disentangle them, an explicit limitation the authors flag rather than resolve.
- Practical methods appendix on using live ad-network analytics as a DV: authors note ad platforms show high-performing ads more, so uneven exposure must be manually corrected/monitored daily; the market for relevant impressions is finite (they state 80 ads was near the ceiling for their keyword niche); and click performance vs. expert rating are different, non-substitutable outcome constructs, offered as a generalizable methodological caution for anyone using real ad-campaign clicks as an HCI outcome measure.

### Shepherding the Crowd: Managing and Providing Feedback to Crowd Workers
- **Authors:** Steven P. Dow, Anand Kulkarni, Scott R. Klemmer, Björn Hartmann
- **Venue/Year:** ACM CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, Extended Abstracts / Workshop (CHI '11) 2011
- **URL:** https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~spdow/files/Crowds-Shepherd-ws-CHI11.pdf
- **Relevance:** Early foundational work on structuring iterative feedback exchange for distributed non-expert contributors, underlies the paper's framing of structured feedback loops between community co-designers and AI/expert review.

**Methodology summary:** Note on identity: the paper's actual byline order is Steven P. Dow, Brie Bunge, Truc Nguyen, Scott R. Klemmer (Stanford HCI Group) and Anand Kulkarni, Björn Hartmann (UC Berkeley), two more co-authors (Bunge, Nguyen) than the citation given in the task. It is a 6-page CHI 2011 Extended Abstracts "Work-in-Progress" paper, not a full paper, this matters for how much empirical weight it can bear. Full PDF retrieved directly from Björn Hartmann's Berkeley site (people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bjoern/papers/dow-shepherd-chi2011-wip.pdf).

Content structure: (1) a conceptual "design space for crowd feedback" with five dimensions, Timeliness (sync/async), Specificity (accept-reject / templates / freeform), Source (requester / peer workers), Format (text/image/audio/video), and Work:Feedback ratio (1:1, many:1, 1:many, many:many); (2) introduction of a prototype system, Shepherd, for visualizing crowd work and delivering feedback, with timeline and matrix dashboard views for requesters; (3) ONE completed preliminary experiment; (4) two additional studies described only as planned future work (not yet run at time of writing).

The completed study: Between-subjects design testing "(How) does synchronous accept-reject feedback improve task performance and worker satisfaction?" Task: write short advertising catchphrases (up to 10, $0.10 each) for a real client; workers could leave anytime. N=58 MTurk workers, unevenly split: 27 in Feedback condition, 31 in No-feedback condition. Feedback condition: within ~1 minute of submission each catchphrase was synchronously labeled Accept or Reject (binary, requester-authored, no explanation given). No-feedback condition: catchphrases simply appeared in a running list, no evaluative label. Output: 243 unique ad ideas total (105 from the smaller Feedback group, 138 from the No-feedback group, difference not significant). Quality measure: 10 independent, blind judges rated every idea on a 20-point scale covering theme, creativity, and professionalism, yielding 2,430 individual ratings. Analysis: two-way ANOVA with Condition (Feedback/No-feedback) and Judge (1 to 10) as factors and rating as the DV. Result: Feedback condition scored higher (μ=9.4, SD=4.7) than No-feedback (μ=8.9, SD=4.6), F(1,2429)=8.65, p<0.05; judges differed significantly from each other too (F(9,2421)=10.41, p<0.05), but 9 of 10 agreed Feedback ads were better. No manipulation check, no inter-rater reliability statistic (e.g., ICC/Krippendorff's alpha) reported, no effect size (e.g., Cohen's d) reported, no attrition/dropout analysis despite workers being free to quit at any time.

**Strengths:**
- Real-stakes ecological validity: uses actual MTurk workers doing genuinely deployed paid tasks (ad catchphrases for a real client, later run through Google AdWords), not a lab simulation.
- Blind, independent multi-rater quality assessment (10 judges, 2,430 ratings) rather than relying on the requester's own (biased) judgment of whether feedback 'worked', a good bias-avoidance move.
- Both quantity and quality outcomes reported, giving a fuller and honestly mixed picture (feedback improved quality but did not increase, if anything lowered, output volume), rather than cherry-picking the flattering metric.
- Explicitly conceptualizes the feedback design space (timeliness, specificity, source, format, ratio) before running experiments, gives the empirical result a reusable theoretical frame beyond the single study, useful for citation as a taxonomy rather than just a data point.
- Transparent about its own incompleteness: clearly labels two of its three studies as future work rather than overclaiming finished results, which is honest EA/WIP practice.

**Weaknesses:**
- Small, unbalanced sample (27 vs. 31) with no reported randomization/balance check or power analysis, a CHI reviewer would flag the study as underpowered/exploratory, appropriate only for a work-in-progress venue, not as standalone evidence.
- No manipulation check and no mechanism isolation: binary Accept/Reject feedback carries almost no informational content, so the observed quality gain is equally consistent with a 'someone is watching/evaluating me' social-facilitation or surveillance effect as with genuine skill learning, the paper cannot distinguish these, despite framing the result as evidence for feedback-driven learning.
- Non-independence of the significance test: treating all 2,430 individual judge-ratings as independent observations in a factorial ANOVA (rather than modeling the nested structure, 243 items × 10 judges, or averaging per item first) is a classic pseudoreplication error that artificially inflates degrees of freedom and statistical power, making the reported p<0.05 look stronger than it is.
- No inter-rater reliability statistic reported (no ICC, Krippendorff's alpha, etc.) despite judges differing significantly from one another (F(9,2421)=10.41, p<0.05), a reviewer would want to know how much the 20-point composite score can be trusted before trusting the condition effect built on top of it.
- No effect size reported (only F and p), the raw means (9.4 vs 8.9 on a 20-point scale) suggest a small effect, but this isn't quantified (e.g., Cohen's d, eta-squared).
- Single task domain (short ad catchphrases) undermines generalizability to other content-creation tasks (e.g., the paper's own stated interest in product reviews, categorization), acknowledged implicitly by the authors proposing a second, different-task study rather than replicating.
- No attrition/dropout analysis, even though the task explicitly allowed workers to leave at any time, differential quitting between conditions could confound both the quantity and quality results (e.g., discouraged/rejected workers self-selecting out, changing the composition of the remaining sample) and is not addressed.
- As a 6-page CHI EA paper, the empirical core is thin: one small study plus a conceptual framework and system description; the fuller, more rigorous version of this work (Dow et al., 'Shepherding the Crowd Yields Better Work,' CSCW 2012) exists and largely supersedes it methodologically, which matters for citation choice.

**Sample size / conditions:** N = 58 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers in one completed between-subjects study: Feedback condition n=27, No-feedback condition n=31 (unequal, no reported randomization-balance check). Outcome corpus: 243 unique catchphrases, each rated by 10 independent judges = 2,430 total quality ratings. Two further studies (requester feedback vs. self-review; peer "shepherd" feedback) are proposed but explicitly not yet conducted in this paper.

**Measures used:** (1) Quantity of content produced (catchphrases submitted per condition: 105 vs. 138, ns). (2) Quality: 20-point scale per idea from 10 blind independent judges, covering theme, creativity, and professionalism (single composite score, sub-dimensions not analyzed separately). No self-report satisfaction/motivation measure despite the research question naming "worker satisfaction." No behavioral/learning measure (e.g., improvement across successive submissions within-subject), the design is purely a between-subjects endpoint comparison, so it cannot show individual workers learning/improving over time, only that groups differ in an aggregate output measure.

**Supplementary material notes:** Two concrete methodological precedents from this paper are worth logging in supplementary materials as things our own study design should address rather than repeat: (1) Mechanism confound, Dow et al.'s binary accept/reject manipulation cannot distinguish 'workers learned from the feedback' from 'workers behaved better because they knew they were being watched/evaluated' (a social-facilitation/surveillance confound); our supplementary methods section should note this as a precedent motivating an explicit manipulation check or a condition that separates 'observed/reviewed' from 'given substantive AI critique' when we evaluate whether AI/expert feedback to community co-designers improves design quality. (2) Rating-independence pitfall, their ANOVA treats every judge×item rating (2,430 of them) as an independent data point despite the nested judges×items structure and reports no inter-rater reliability statistic; this is a specific, citable methodological misstep to avoid, and belongs in supplementary as a rationale for why we will use mixed-effects/multilevel models (or report ICC/Krippendorff's alpha) when multiple raters score community-generated 3D designs in our own evaluation.

**Cite in main paper:** Cite it in the main related-work section as the foundational empirical demonstration that even minimal, synchronous, structured feedback measurably improves the quality of open-ended creative output from distributed non-expert contributors, directly underwriting the paper's framing of structured feedback loops between community co-designers and AI/expert review (though prefer citing/also citing the more rigorous CSCW 2012 'Shepherding the Crowd Yields Better Work' journal version alongside it, since that is the maturer, higher-powered version of the same claim).

**Cite in supplementary:** The paper's specific analysis choices (unmodeled non-independence of multi-rater ratings; no isolation of surveillance-effect vs. learning-effect as the causal mechanism behind the feedback benefit) are useful negative methodological precedents to cite explicitly in our supplementary materials, justifying stronger statistical and design choices in our own multi-rater, feedback-loop evaluation.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "The Feedback condition (µ=9.4, SD=4.7) outperformed the no feedback condition (µ=8.9, SD=4.6) (F(1,2429)=8.65, p<0.05)."
- "The No feedback condition produced more ideas overall (138 to 105), but the difference was not significant."
- "While overall ratings differed significantly between judges (F(9,2421)=10.41, p<0.05), all but one agreed that Feedback ads were better."
- "How can crowdsourcing platforms motivate and scaffold novice workers to improve over time, especially on complex, large-scale, creative tasks? We hypothesize that worker interaction with requesters and with other workers is a key missing component."
- "Currently, platforms like Mechanical Turk only allow asynchronous feedback with no enticement to return... unless requesters have more jobs available, workers cannot act on requesters' advice."
- The paper is explicit that this is preliminary/in-progress: two of three planned studies (requester-vs-self-review comparison; peer 'shepherd' effectiveness) are stated as future work, not executed.

### SpaceTime: Enabling Fluid Individual and Collaborative Editing in Virtual Reality
- **Authors:** Haijun Xia, Sebastian Herscher, Ken Perlin, Daniel Wigdor
- **Venue/Year:** UIST 2018 (Proceedings of the 31st Annual ACM Symposium on User Interface Software and Technology), pp. 853 to 866 2018
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3242587.3242597
- **Relevance:** The canonical Xia paper on multi-user 3D/spatial collaborative editing. Introduces Container, Parallel Objects, and Avatar Objects to let multiple users manipulate a shared 3D scene fluidly without stepping on each other's individual agency -- directly relevant to the multi-user co-design layer of the Pascal-based tool.

- **Relevance:** Extends task-driven malleable UI generation to orchestration across many interfaces at once, combining user direction with AI facilitation -- relevant analogy for orchestrating multiple design proposals/constraints in a community co-design session.

**Methodology summary:** Formative/exploratory lab study (not a controlled experiment). Authors explicitly state they did NOT include a separate control/baseline condition, reasoning that participants' existing daily experience with tabbed browsing (and, informally, with AI search tools like ChatGPT/Perplexity) already functions as their de facto baseline for judging Orca. The study is organized around 5 pre-registered-style research questions (RQ1: exploration/sensemaking/retrieval; RQ2: spatial-canvas navigation and challenges; RQ3: system status legibility, expressing needs, human-AI collaboration; RQ4: subjective comparison to AI-driven search engines; RQ5: identifying/recovering from AI failures). Each RQ has a corresponding findings subsection (7.3.1-7.3.5) blending Likert tallies, interview quotes, and observed usage behavior, followed by an explicit Limitations subsection (7.3.6).\n\nProcedure: single 60-minute in-person session per participant ($20 Amazon gift card), structured as Introduction (5 min) → semi-structured walkthrough/tutorial with example tasks (30 min, experimenter-guided) → freeform task of the participant's own choosing (15 min) → post-session 5-point Likert questionnaire + semi-structured interview (10 min).\n\nSample: N=8 (4 female, 4 male, ages 21-27), recruited via 'internal channels' at a single university. All were regular generative-AI users; canvas/graphics-tool experience (Figma, Illustrator) varied from frequent (4 participants) to rare (2 participants) to occasional (2 participants). Single institution, single demographic band, single session per person, no longitudinal or repeated-measures component.\n\nMeasures: (1) a custom, non-named/non-validated 5-point Likert questionnaire on unspecified 'system dimensions and overall experience' reported only as raw agree/strongly-agree counts per RQ; (2) semi-structured interview transcripts mined for illustrative quotes; (3) informal behavioral trace metrics (e.g., average number of webpages accumulated on the canvas by session end ≈ 37).\n\nAnalysis approach: purely qualitative/descriptive, findings are presented as five named themes tied to the RQs, supported by representative quotes and blunt Likert counts. No named qualitative coding procedure (no thematic analysis, affinity diagramming, codebook, or inter-rater reliability described), and no inferential statistics (appropriate for N=8, but not discussed as a deliberate scoping choice beyond the small sample size itself).

**Strengths:**
- Explicit, argued rationale for omitting a control condition (leverages participants' entrenched real-world tabbed-browsing/AI-search habits as the implicit comparator) rather than silently skipping one, a scoping decision CHI reviewers respect when justified, as here, versus simply absent.
- Genuine mixed-methods triangulation: subjective Likert ratings + semi-structured interview quotes + observed behavioral trace (page-accumulation counts) cross-validate the self-report data.
- Findings are cleanly organized 1:1 against pre-specified research questions, giving the evaluation a legible, falsifiable structure rather than an undifferentiated list of anecdotes.
- Reports concrete, feature-grounded failure/recovery episodes in detail (e.g., P4's Google-Flights URL-parameter failure and workaround) rather than only positive results, shows breakdowns as well as successes.
- Transparent, specific self-reported limitation (short exposure time, ~37 pages/session) rather than a generic boilerplate limitations paragraph, which strengthens credibility and gives future work (including this project) a citable precedent.

**Weaknesses:**
- Very small (N=8), single-institution, narrow-demographic sample (ages 21-27, recruited via internal university channels, all already comfortable GenAI users), threatens generalizability to non-expert, older, or less tech-savvy 'community' populations that a civic co-design tool would actually serve.
- No true baseline/control condition: the 'comparison' to tabbed browsing and AI search tools is entirely retrospective self-report during interview, not a within-subjects task performed on a comparison system in the same session, introduces recall bias and cannot rule out novelty/demand-characteristic effects inflating reported enthusiasm ('appetite for exploration').
- Only 15 minutes of unguided, participant-chosen freeform task time immediately following a 30-minute experimenter-guided tutorial, tasks are unstandardized across participants (each chose their own), so freeform-phase results are not comparable across participants, and priming from the guided walkthrough likely shapes freeform behavior.
- The post-session questionnaire is custom and unvalidated: no items, subscales, reliability, or citation of an established instrument (e.g., SUS, NASA-TLX) are given, and results are reported only as raw agree/strongly-agree counts with no variance, not reusable or benchmarkable by other researchers.
- No described qualitative analysis procedure: no coding scheme, no thematic-analysis or affinity-diagramming method named, no mention of number of coders or inter-rater reliability, the quotes read as illustrative selections rather than the output of a transparent, auditable analysis process.
- Single 60-minute session per participant precludes any assessment of learning curve, retention, or habituation to a genuinely new interaction paradigm (spatial canvas + parallel AI agents); the authors' own limitation (short exposure time) is acknowledged but not mitigated by even a short follow-up or diary component.
- No inferential statistics or effect-size reporting (defensible given N=8, but the paper does not explicitly frame this as a deliberate formative-study choice beyond noting the small sample in passing).

Single condition, no control/baseline group, authors explicitly substitute participants' pre-existing daily tabbed-browsing (and informal AI-search-tool) experience as the comparative baseline rather than running a separate condition. One 60-minute in-person session per participant ($20 gift card): intro (5 min) → guided walkthrough/tutorial (30 min) → self-chosen freeform task (15 min) → Likert questionnaire + semi-structured interview (10 min).

**Measures used:** Custom (unvalidated, unnamed) 5-point Likert-scale post-study questionnaire on system dimensions/overall experience, reported as raw response-count tallies per research question; semi-structured post-task interview analyzed for representative quotes (no described coding scheme); informal behavioral/usage trace (e.g., mean pages accumulated on canvas by session end).

**Supplementary material notes:** No separate supplementary/appendix evaluation materials were found beyond the main text; the paper's appendices (A: constructing feedforward option lists from parallel LLM responses; B: HTML distillation for web automation/page extraction) are technical/system-implementation appendices, not additional study data, questionnaires, or statistical supplements. **Cite in main paper:** Orca operationalizes 'user-driven, AI-facilitated orchestration' across many malleable artifacts (webpages) while explicitly preserving human control over AI agents acting in parallel, a direct design/theoretical analogy for orchestrating multiple design proposals, constraints, and AI facilitation threads in a multi-user community co-design session, and it comes from the same lab (Xia) as this paper's malleable/generative-UI theoretical grounding.

**Cite in supplementary:** Its explicit, reasoned justification for omitting a control condition (using participants' entrenched daily-use baseline as the implicit comparator) plus its candidly stated single-session exposure-time limitation (mean 37 pages accumulated in one 60-minute session, insufficient to reach steady-state use) is a directly reusable methodological precedent for justifying a similarly small-N, single-session, no-control formative pilot with community co-design participants, and for pre-empting the same 'not enough exposure time' reviewer critique.

- 5-point Likert results are reported only as raw agree/strongly-agree counts (e.g., 'RQ1: 3 strongly agree and 5 agree'; 'RQ4: 2 strongly agree, 6 agree') with no item wording, scale validation, or variance reported.

### Belidor: A Specification Language for Operationalizing Structural Analogies Between User Interfaces
- **Authors:** Matthew T. Beaudouin-Lafon, Devamardeep Hayatpur, Arvind Satyanarayan, Haijun Xia
- **Venue/Year:** CHI 2026 (ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems) 2026
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791613
- **Relevance:** Formalizes reusable structural analogies between generative interfaces, part of Xia's malleable-UI research line; useful for arguing a principled generation grammar behind AI-assisted 3D design edits rather than ad hoc prompt-to-geometry mapping.

**Methodology summary:** This is a systems/specification-language contribution paper, not an empirical human-subjects study. Belidor is a YAML-embedded, entity-relationship DSL that represents a UI's conceptual model, presentation (marks/encodings), and behavior (CRUD actions) with a small set of primitive relations (affects>, cover>, group>, subset>, mapto>, alias>, create/update/delete>, directions>), motivated by Gentner's Structure Mapping Theory (analogies are strongest when they map relations, especially higher-order relations, not surface attributes). Validation is entirely author-driven and threefold: (1) an "example gallery" of ~10 hand-authored specs spanning GUIs (messaging, video editor, calendar, color picker, code editor, file explorer, spreadsheet, Figma/PowerPoint, web browser/CAD) plus non-GUI modalities (screen reader, elevator hardware) to demonstrate expressivity; (2) a computational analogy-finding method: specs are compiled to directed graphs and an off-the-shelf exact/approximate Graph Edit Distance algorithm (Abu-Aisheh et al. 2015, via NetworkX) with manually-tuned edit-cost functions is run on all 45 pairwise combinations of the 10 specs (2-hour timeout per pair, results flagged with an asterisk if fewer than 4 improving iterations were found, i.e. search likely didn't converge); (3) three qualitative case studies (Calendar↔Video Editor, Figma↔PowerPoint, Web Browser↔CAD "tabs" design pattern) where the authors manually align specifications to reveal analogies, propose concrete feature-transfer ideas (e.g., calendar appointment slots → video-editor placeholder clips; DaVinci adjustment clips → calendar bulk-meeting edits), and abstract a reusable "design pattern" specification. Additionally, the authors self-administer Green et al.'s Cognitive Dimensions of Notation framework (premature commitment, abstraction gradient, viscosity, hidden dependencies, secondary notation) as a structured internal usability reflection on the language design, rather than testing it with external users. There is no sample of human participants, no between/within-subjects conditions, no quantitative measures with statistical tests, and no ground truth against which "correct" analogies are checked other than the authors' own judgment. A public interactive "Analogy Viewer" (belidor-specification.github.io) is released for inspection/reproducibility.

**Strengths:**
- Strong, explicit theoretical grounding: the language's design decisions (reifying structure as first-class, treating higher-order relations specially) are directly derived from and justified against Gentner's Structure Mapping Theory, giving the systems contribution a principled cognitive-science rationale rather than ad hoc design.
- Multi-pronged triangulation for a systems/language paper: expressivity gallery + algorithmic analogy-finding + hand-worked case studies + a structured Cognitive Dimensions of Notation self-assessment, which is a well-regarded, reviewer-legible validation pattern for PL/HCI notation contributions (in the tradition of Grammar of Graphics, Bluefish, Interaction Substrates).
- Unusually transparent and well-reasoned limitations sections (3.8.3, 5.1.3, 6.1): the authors proactively name what the language deliberately excludes (input bindings, modes, computation) and why, and correctly invoke Olsen (2007) to justify why a 'walk up and use' human study would not fit a language contribution, this preempts an obvious reviewer objection rather than hiding from it.
- Released a public, interactive artifact (the Analogy Viewer) that lets readers/reviewers independently inspect the 45-pair analogy results, supporting scrutiny and reproducibility beyond the static figures.
- Careful, well-scoped related-work positioning distinguishing Belidor from MBUID, Concept Design, Interaction Substrates, and MVC, situates the structural/relational approach as a genuine gap rather than reinventing prior specification languages.

**Weaknesses:**
- Zero human-subjects evaluation: no evidence that practicing designers can read, write, or benefit from Belidor specs, or that the analogies it surfaces are actually useful/understandable to anyone but the four authors, the central claim ('Belidor serves as an effective representation for structural analogies') is validated entirely by the inventors' own judgment.
- Confirmation-bias risk in the case studies and gallery: the ~10 specifications and 3 case-study pairs were hand-selected and hand-written by the authors specifically to make the analogy argument work, not sampled from an independent or representative corpus of UIs, a reviewer would flag this as classic 'gallery of examples' cherry-picking with no claim of generalizability.
- The graph-edit-distance cost function was 'manually tuned, although not extensively' with no ablation, held-out validation set, or sensitivity analysis reported, since the same authors both tuned the costs and judged whether the resulting matches were 'reasonable,' the quantitative analogy scores are not independent evidence of anything beyond internal consistency.
- The algorithm's marquee success stories are undercut by their own reporting: for Figma/PowerPoint the free-running algorithm found nothing, and the paper's positive result came from manually seeding the expected answer and showing it scored lower cost, this is not a demonstration that the algorithm discovers analogies, only that Belidor's representation can express a pre-conceived one at lower cost, a weaker and more circular claim than the framing suggests.
- No comparison against the very baselines the paper motivates itself against (screenshot-based ML methods like Misty/Screen Correspondence, or DOM-retargeting like Bricolage) on any shared task or interface, the paper argues Belidor is better for structural (vs. visual) analogies but never empirically pits the two approaches against each other on the same UIs.
- The 45-pair matrix is derived from only 10 underlying specifications, so the pairs are not independent data points; combined with per-pair 2-hour timeouts, NP-hard non-convergence, and asterisked 'inadequate search' pairs, the quantitative-looking Figure 12 heatmap has much less evidential weight than its polished presentation implies.

**Sample size / conditions:** No human participants. The "corpus" is 10 author-written Belidor specifications of real applications, yielding all 45 unique pairwise comparisons for the algorithmic analysis, plus 3 hand-selected pairs used as in-depth qualitative case studies. No control condition, no baseline comparison against the screenshot- or DOM-based analogy methods (Bricolage, Misty, Screen Correspondence) that the paper's own related work section critiques.

**Measures used:** (1) Graph edit cost / minimum-cost edit path between two compiled specification graphs (nodes = objects/structures/types, edges = labeled relations), with hand-tuned penalty/reward weights (e.g., penalize cross-layer matches like gui↔action, reward matching structure shapes). (2) A derived "conceptual overlap" percentage = proportion of edges in/at the boundary of the smaller specification's conceptual layer that appear in the found analogy, used to rank pairs in the Figure 12 heatmap. (3) An "iteration count" convergence flag (asterisk if <4 improving iterations within the 2-hour cutoff) as an informal proxy for search adequacy. (4) Cognitive Dimensions of Notation (Green & Petre 1996) applied qualitatively/introspectively by the authors to the language itself, not to user behavior.

**Supplementary material notes:** No separate supplementary/appendix document was found distinct from the 21-page CHI '26 camera-ready PDF itself (hosted at hci.ucsd.edu/papers/belidor.pdf and vis.mit.edu/pubs/belidor.pdf); the paper's own 'supplementary-like' artifact is the public interactive Analogy Viewer at https://belidor-specification.github.io/, which hosts the 45-pair algorithmic results and lets a reader explore individual analogies, this is the closest thing to supplementary material and is itself methodologically interesting (a released tool for scrutinizing the algorithm's output rather than only static tables).

**Cite in main paper:** Cite Belidor in the MAIN paper's related work: it is the most directly relevant precedent (same lab, Xia co-authored, CHI 2026) for arguing that AI-assisted generative edits to an interface/design artifact should be grounded in an explicit, relational structural grammar of the underlying conceptual/presentation/behavior model rather than in ad hoc, surface-level prompt-to-output mapping, directly supporting our claim that voice/AI-assisted 3D architectural edits need a principled generation grammar, not ad hoc prompt-to-geometry translation.

**Cite in supplementary:** Belidor's own methodological handling of the 'we can't run a walk-up-and-use study on a language/grammar contribution' problem (explicitly invoking Olsen 2007, and instead validating via a gallery + algorithmic proxy + self-administered Cognitive Dimensions of Notation) is a useful precedent to cite in OUR supplementary materials if our AI-assisted 3D generation grammar component similarly lacks a full end-to-end user study, it shows CHI reviewers accept (and even reward) this substitution pattern for systems/language contributions, provided the limitation is stated as transparently as Belidor does, and it also flags a trap to avoid: don't let the only 'validation' of whether the grammar's outputs are good be the inventors' own post-hoc judgment of a hand-picked case study, as Belidor's Figma/PowerPoint result illustrates.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "We strongly penalize matching nodes across conceptual, presentation and behavior layers... These costs were manually tuned, although not extensively." (Section 5.1.1), an explicit admission of untested researcher degrees of freedom in the core algorithm.
- For Figma/PowerPoint, the unconstrained 2-hour algorithm run found "no meaningful analogy" (0% conceptual match); the authors then manually seeded the algorithm with their own expected match and showed it scored a lower edit cost (9.5% lower) and 37% conceptual overlap, i.e., the paper's headline 'discovery' for that pair was validated by confirming a human-authored hypothesis rather than by the algorithm autonomously finding it.
- "Evaluating UI toolkits, let alone theoretical frameworks, is notoriously difficult [Olsen 2007]... we expect designers would need time to internalize the language's syntax and semantics. Therefore, studying how people use a language requires longer term upfront training and results in rich qualitative observations, both of which are beyond the scope of this paper." (Section 6.1), the authors explicitly and self-consciously scope out any human evaluation, deferring it to future work.
- The GED problem is NP-hard and the chosen algorithm (depth-first search ordered by Munkres' algorithm) 'can get trapped in suboptimal branches... possibly before we terminate the search', acknowledged non-convergence risk baked into the central quantitative artifact (Figure 12).

### Gazeify Then Voiceify: Physical Object Referencing Through Gaze and Voice Interaction with Displayless Smart Glasses
- **Authors:** Zheng Zhang, Mengjie Yu, Tianyi Wang, Kashyap Todi, Ajoy Savio Fernandes, Yue Liu, Haijun Xia, Tovi Grossman, Tanya R. Jonker
- **Venue/Year:** IUI 2026 (ACM Conference on Intelligent User Interfaces) 2026
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3742413.3789112
- **Relevance:** Xia's most direct voice-plus-multimodal interaction paper: combines gaze-based referencing with voice description to disambiguate and act on physical/spatial objects -- directly analogous to voice commands referencing specific 3D building elements in a co-design scene.

**Methodology summary:** A two-stage multimodal referencing pipeline for displayless smart glasses: (1) "Gazeify", a spatiotemporal-aware gaze sampler clusters raw gaze points on color, depth, location, and velocity, and passes the centroid to EfficientSAM to segment the fixated object into three candidate masks with confidence scores; (2) "Voiceify", GPT-4o generates a natural-language description of the selected object using a fixed template ("I've selected [adjective properties] [object identity] that [spatial/ordinal relation to nearby objects]"), which the user hears and can correct via a follow-up voice command. Voice-driven disambiguation runs a three-stage recovery pipeline: object detection (SAM + RT-DETR), VLM/heuristic bounding-box filtering, and VLM-based candidate ranking/localization. Evaluated in a within-subjects lab study manipulating three factors, object size (small vs. normal visual angle), environmental clutter (cluttered vs. clean), and referential ambiguity (structural, positional, or none), yielding 12 conditions x 3 trials = 36 trials/participant in a staged "coffee shop" environment with four object zones. Each trial: gaze-select, hear generated description, and (if wrong) issue up to two rounds of corrective voice commands. Analysis combined repeated-measures ANOVA (with effect sizes) for the three manipulated factors on gaze accuracy, linear mixed-effects models with participant as random effect for interaction/robustness checks, manual error-type coding (taxonomies of gaze errors, description errors, and disambiguation-failure errors), and standard post-study instruments (SUS, NASA-TLX, custom Likert items) plus semi-structured interviews analyzed qualitatively for themes (strengths, limitations, use cases).

**Strengths:**
- Well-powered, tightly controlled 3-factor within-subject factorial design (size x clutter x ambiguity) with proper repeated-measures ANOVA and reported effect sizes (ηp²), letting them attribute accuracy drops to specific, theoretically motivated conditions rather than reporting a single aggregate number -- a CHI reviewer would praise this as rigorous, hypothesis-driven manipulation checking.
- Mixed quantitative + qualitative triangulation: objective mask-accuracy metrics, precision/recall/F1 on voice description correctness, plus validated instruments (SUS, NASA-TLX) and semi-structured interviews, giving both performance and subjective-experience evidence.
- Detailed, granular error taxonomies (4 gaze-error types, 4 description-error types, 6 disambiguation-failure types) built from manual coding of failures -- this turns 'the system got 53% right' into an actionable diagnostic breakdown, which is unusually thorough and directly reusable by other researchers designing correction UX.
- Realistic, ecologically-motivated test environment (staged coffee-shop scene with four object zones, cluttered/clean and near/far manipulations) rather than a sterile lab task, improving external validity for real-world smart-glasses use.
- Transparent, precise reporting of eye-tracking calibration quality (1.16°±0.22°) as a controlled precondition, which strengthens confidence that gaze-accuracy failures reflect the referencing pipeline rather than uncalibrated hardware.

**Weaknesses:**
- No baseline/comparison condition -- the authors themselves acknowledge they did not compare against any alternative technique (e.g., manual pointing, dwell-only gaze, touch-based selection), which a CHI reviewer would flag as limiting claims that gaze+voice is actually *better* than simpler alternatives, only that it 'works to some degree.'
- Moderate, fairly homogeneous sample (N=23, company-affiliated recruitment pool skewing toward tech-literate participants) constrains generalizability to broader/non-expert user populations -- relevant caution for community-design contexts.
- The core gaze-selection accuracy is quite low in absolute terms (53%), and the paper leans on voice disambiguation to recover majority of the remainder (58.4% of the 409 error trials) -- meaning roughly 22% of all trials likely end without a correct selection even after two disambiguation rounds; a critical reviewer could note the headline system reliability is more modest than the framing suggests.
- Single fixed voice-description template (adjective+identity+spatial relation via GPT-4o) may not generalize to more free-form or domain-specific referencing needs (e.g., architectural/zoning vocabulary), and the paper doesn't stress-test template robustness across object types beyond the study's four locations.
- Explicit limitations admitted by authors -- single-object-only selection, degraded performance beyond 3m, and VLM hallucination -- are real constraints not fully resolved in the reported system, and no mitigation data (e.g., hallucination frequency) is quantified beyond a qualitative note.

**Sample size / conditions:** N=23 participants (14 male, 8 female, 1 undisclosed), recruited from an internal company email list and external participant pools, varying AR/VR and generative-AI experience; mean eye-calibration error 1.16 deg +/- 0.22 deg (calibration target <0.5 deg). Within-subject design, 3 factors x 2/2/3 levels = 12 conditions, 3 trials each = 36 trials/participant (828 trials total across the study, though the disambiguation sub-analysis operates on the subset of 409 trials that required a correction). Sessions ~1 hour, randomized condition order, two practice trials before the main task, max two rounds of voice disambiguation per trial.

**Measures used:** Quantitative: gaze referencing accuracy (mask covers >=90% of object's 2D area, binary correct/incorrect per trial); voice description accuracy via precision/recall/F1 on whether participants correctly judged the spoken description as right/wrong; voice disambiguation success rate (post-correction mask accuracy, max 2 rounds), decomposed into genuine-error corrections vs. false-negative recoveries, plus single- vs. two-round success rates. Manual error taxonomies: 4 gaze-error types (part-of, more-than, other-object, background), 4 description-error types (part-whole, relative position, semantic, mask coverage), 6 disambiguation-failure types (object detection, filtering, command quality, speech recognition, model comprehension, localization). Subjective/qualitative: System Usability Scale (SUS), NASA-TLX (mental/physical demand, frustration, difficulty, temporal pressure), custom Likert ratings (usefulness, naturalness, effectiveness), and semi-structured post-study interviews.

**Supplementary material notes:** Two methodological precedents worth citing in OUR supplementary materials: (1) their layered error taxonomy (separate typologies for referencing errors, description errors, and disambiguation-failure errors) is a strong template for how we should categorize failures when voice commands target ambiguous 3D building elements (e.g., 'part-of' vs. 'other-object' vs. 'background' confusions map directly onto selecting a wall segment vs. an adjacent room vs. empty space); and (2) their explicit acknowledged limitation of omitting a baseline comparison ('the task... lacks existing baselines for comparison') is a useful precedent to cite if we face the same reviewer critique -- i.e., we can note that novel multimodal referencing tasks in HCI commonly lack established baselines, and describe how we mitigate this (e.g., via within-subject conditions or expert-baseline proxies) in our own study design section.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the main paper's related-work section: it is Xia's most directly analogous multimodal precedent -- combining gaze-based object referencing with voice description/correction to disambiguate physical objects -- which maps closely onto voice commands referencing specific 3D building elements in a shared co-design scene, even though the domain (AR smart glasses) differs from multi-user 3D architectural editing.

**Cite in supplementary:** The granular error-taxonomy method and the field's accepted practice of omitting baselines for genuinely novel multimodal referencing tasks are concrete methodological precedents (not core motivating literature) best placed in supplementary material to justify our own error-coding scheme and study-design choices without cluttering the main related-work narrative.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Overall gaze referencing accuracy was 53% (SD=13%), with significant main effects of object size (F(1,22)=15.60, p<.001, ηp²=.415), clutter (F(1,22)=4.79, p<.05, ηp²=.179), and ambiguity type (F(2,44)=4.51, p<.05, ηp²=.170).
- Voice description judgment accuracy: precision .86, recall .82, F1 .84; participants perceived 83.6% (92/110) of descriptions without ground-truth boxes as accurate.
- Of 409 trials requiring disambiguation, voice commands successfully produced a correct mask in 58.4% (239/409) of cases -- 43.3% correcting genuine errors, 15.2% recovering false negatives; single-command success rate 87.0% vs. two-round 13%.
- SUS = 73.70 (SD=16.56), rated as 'good usability'; NASA-TLX showed low mental/physical demand and frustration.
- Authors' stated limitations: system selects only a single object as a reference at a time, performs poorly at long distances (>3m), and occasionally exhibits VLM hallucination.
- The study explicitly did not include a baseline/comparison condition, stating the task 'is novel and lacks existing baselines for comparison.'

### Making Abstraction Concrete: A Design Space and Interaction Model of Abstraction in Interactive Systems
- **Authors:** Bryan Min, Sangho Suh, Jim Hollan, Haijun Xia
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv preprint (HCI) 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11344
- **Relevance:** Develops an interaction model for moving between abstract and concrete representations in interactive systems -- theoretically useful for framing how non-expert community members move between plain-language intent, voice commands, and concrete zoning-compliant 3D geometry.

**Methodology summary:** This is not an empirical/user study paper, it is a systematic literature survey + theory-construction paper. Methodology (Section 4.1): (1) Gathering: systematic literature search across three databases (ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar) using search terms "abstraction," "abstracting," "abstracted," "abstractive" across 7 core HCI venues (CHI, UIST, DIS, IUI, TOCHI, PACMHCI, TIIS) plus 4 adjacent venues (ASSETS, VL/HCC, TVCG, EuroVIS). Extended abstracts, demos, posters, workshop papers excluded from the start. Merging duplicates yielded 3,128 papers. (2) Filtering: primary inclusion criterion = "contributes an interactive system AND uses abstraction as a design approach." Excluded empirical studies, surveys, technical/performance contributions, meta-research/software-engineering frameworks, and papers using "abstraction" in irrelevant contexts (8 documented sub-categories in Appendix A.1, e.g., term used to describe a real-life cognitive phenomenon, used only in related-work description of another paper's system, used by study participants rather than authors, appearing in "Abstract" section header, etc.). Final corpus: 457 papers. (3) Tagging/coding: iterative codebook built via thematic analysis (citing Braun & Clarke 2006), with two code classes, (a) how authors described their use of "abstraction" as a design approach, (b) what interface design elements resulted. Codes were developed iteratively with the author team discussing emergent patterns/dimensions (no reported inter-rater reliability metric, no fixed number of coders stated precisely, described as a collaborative/discursive process rather than independent double-coding). Output: six design-space dimensions (Unit of Interest, Granularity, Representation, Transformability, Presentation, Guidance) and four design goals mapped onto Norman's stages of action. These empirical findings are then used qualitatively/argumentatively to motivate and construct a new theoretical interaction model, "Abstraction Spaces," which reframes Norman's Gulfs of Execution/Evaluation by adding a "Gulf of Abstraction," "overshoot/undershoot" misalignment categories, and a non-linear "space of abstractions." The model is validated only by applying it retrospectively to re-describe four existing system workflows (vi text editor automaticity, Draco, LLM sensemaking, WritLarge) and two existing interaction models/frameworks (Instrumental Interaction, Gulf of Envisioning), a qualitative "analytical demonstration," not an experiment.

**Strengths:**
- Large, systematically gathered corpus (3,128 → 457 papers) with a fully transparent, itemized, and reusable exclusion-criteria taxonomy (Appendix A.1), a genuine methodological contribution beyond the theory itself; a CHI reviewer would likely praise this rigor for a qualitative/conceptual paper.
- Grounds a new theoretical construct (Gulf of Abstraction) in an explicit synthesis of prior canonical models (Gulfs of Execution/Evaluation, Instrumental Interaction, Sensemaking model, Gulf of Envisioning), showing clear lineage and integrative power rather than inventing terminology in a vacuum.
- The design space taxonomy (6 dimensions) is illustrated with dozens of concrete, well-cited systems, giving it strong face validity and reuse value as a vocabulary/checklist for designers.
- Self-aware about the limits of its own contribution, explicitly states the model is 'not without limitations' and flags that empirical/practical validation is future work, which is intellectually honest and pre-empts obvious reviewer critique.
- Directly and explicitly engages with LLM/voice/NL interaction as a motivating case (Gulf of Envisioning, voice command overshoot example), making it unusually well-aligned, off the shelf, with community-design voice-interaction framing.

**Weaknesses:**
- No empirical validation whatsoever of the proposed model: the six-dimension design space and the Abstraction Spaces model are validated only by the authors' own selective retrospective re-description of 4 systems and 2 frameworks, a classic 'existence proof' rather than a test. A CHI reviewer would flag this as the paper's central weakness: the model could explain almost anything post hoc, and no falsifiable claim or comparison group is offered.
- The qualitative coding process is thinly described: no inter-rater reliability, no reported number of independent coders, no coding disagreement resolution protocol, just 'we frequently discussed emerging patterns.' This is standard for many HCI design-space papers but is a soft spot next to the otherwise rigorous corpus-gathering methodology.
- Search terms are lexical ("abstraction," "abstracting," "abstracted," "abstractive"), this guarantees high recall for papers that use the word but risks missing systems that instantiate abstraction techniques without using that vocabulary (a systematic blind spot the authors do not discuss).
- The authors admit the exclusion criteria plus filtering process introduce substantial subjective judgment (e.g., distinguishing 'relevant' vs. 'irrelevant' uses of the term) that is not independently verified.
- The final theoretical model's core visual metaphor (a 2D plane / vertical gulf) is acknowledged by the authors themselves to feel 'constraining' (Sec 7.3), an unresolved tension between the paper's own '857 space' argument and the representation it uses to argue for that space.
- No engagement with non-English-language systems, non-academic industry systems, or community/civic-facing design contexts, the survey is confined to top-tier HCI venues, so applicability to non-expert, community co-design contexts (as opposed to expert-built research systems) is untested.

**Sample size / conditions:** No participants, no experimental conditions, no quantitative measures. The "sample" is a corpus of scholarly papers: 3,128 papers gathered → 457 papers retained after filtering (the analytic unit). There is no user study, no A/B conditions, no counterbalancing, no statistical tests. Demonstration of the resulting model is via 4 illustrative existing-system case analyses plus 2 existing-framework comparisons, purely qualitative/rhetorical, chosen by the authors, not sampled systematically.

**Measures used:** No quantitative measures (no scales, no reliability statistics, no coding agreement scores reported). The "measure" is qualitative: a codebook derived through thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke 2006) with two code classes (rhetorical use of "abstraction" as design approach; resulting interface design elements). The paper explicitly documents its exclusion/inclusion criteria as a de facto methodological instrument (Appendix A.1), which is itself a notable methodological artifact, a fully itemized taxonomy of "irrelevant" uses of a keyword, useful as a precedent for disciplined term-based corpus curation.

**Supplementary material notes:** The only "supplementary" content is Appendix A.1 (Exclusion Criteria for the Survey), which is included in the main PDF itself (arXiv:2605.11344v1) rather than as a separate supplementary file. It itemizes 8 specific irrelevant-usage patterns for the keyword "abstraction" that were excluded during filtering (e.g., describing a real-life cognitive phenomenon, describing a different paper's system in related work, appearing only in the word "Abstract" as a section header, appearing in references/copyright sections, used by study participants rather than authors). No separate supplementary PDF, dataset, or code repository was found/linked in the paper.

**Cite in main paper:** Cite in the main paper's related-work/framing section as the theoretical lens for how non-expert community members traverse plain-language intent → voice commands → concrete zoning-compliant 3D geometry: the paper's "Gulf of Abstraction" and "overshoot/undershoot" vocabulary gives a ready-made, HCI-legitimated way to describe and diagnose exactly the alignment failures a voice-first, AI-assisted co-design tool will encounter (e.g., a community member's vague intent "put more green space here" overshooting what the system's zoning-compliant geometry can resolve, or undershooting when the system demands overly granular parametric input).

**Cite in supplementary:** Cite as a methodological precedent in supplementary materials, specifically for two things: (1) their Appendix A.1 exclusion-criteria taxonomy as a template if our own supplementary material needs to justify a related-work/formative corpus search methodology; and (2) their explicitly stated limitation, that a novel interaction-model metaphor was proposed but "its boundaries remain untested" and requires "broader evaluations... applying it to a wider range of systems", as a citable precedent for why our own paper should include (or justify not yet including) an empirical validation of any new interaction/abstraction model we propose, rather than resting solely on illustrative case walkthroughs.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "We surveyed 457 papers and synthesized a design space of abstraction techniques along six dimensions." (Abstract)
- Six dimensions: Unit of Interest, Granularity, Representation, Transformability, Presentation, Guidance (Sec 4.3).
- New theoretical construct: "Gulf of Abstraction", the misalignment between user mental-model abstractions and system-provided abstractions, plus two failure modes: "Abstraction Overshoots" (system/user abstraction too high relative to the other) and "Abstraction Undershoots" (too low).
- Explicit worked example directly relevant to voice interaction: "a user instructs a system using voice commands, but cannot steer it precisely enough using this modality... they may reach cases where these intents are not recognized accurately", framed as an abstraction overshoot (Sec 5.2).
- Self-acknowledged limitation (Sec 7.1): "our interaction model is not without limitations... its boundaries remain untested. We are particularly interested in conducting broader evaluations of this metaphor in practice, especially when applying it to a wider range of systems.", i.e., the authors themselves flag that the model has had no empirical/user validation.
- Appendix A.1 gives a fully itemized exclusion-criteria taxonomy for a keyword-based literature survey, a reusable methodological template.
- Explicitly discusses LLM-era relevance: reframes the 'Gulf of Envisioning' (Subramonyam et al. 2024), the distance between a user's intention and the prompt they construct for an LLM, as a case where the system's abstraction level is an unpredictable 'moving target,' directly analogous to voice/NL command interpretation problems.

## Cluster: Voice-first & non-visual interfaces for spatial/CAD design

### MozArt: A Multimodal Interface for Conceptual 3D Modeling
- **Authors:** Anirudh Sharma, Sriganesh Madhvanath, Ankit Shekhawat, Mark Billinghurst
- **Venue/Year:** ICMI 2011 (13th International Conference on Multimodal Interfaces) 2011
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2070481.2070538
- **Relevance:** Combines speech and multitouch for conceptual/architectural 3D modeling with a button-less interface; directly relevant precedent for voice-driven 3D authoring by non-experts, and for arguing the design space of speech+direct-manipulation in a Pascal-based co-design tool.

**Methodology summary:** Within-subjects lab pilot study comparing a custom multimodal (speech + multitouch) interface against a conventional multitouch-only (toolbar/button) interface for conceptual 3D modeling. Both interfaces are layered on top of Google SketchUp, run on a custom-built tiltable "drafting table" prototype using IR-laser multitouch sensing (TUIO via Community Core Vision) and CMUSphinx speech recognition; recognized speech/touch events are mapped via a Ruby/SketchUp add-on to SketchUp's keyboard-shortcut commands. The multimodal vocabulary is small and fixed: 2 touch gestures (pinch-zoom, single-touch drag) and 8 speech commands ("Circle," "Rectangle," "Line," "Pull it up," "Show Isometric," "Top View," "Show me around," "Undo"), each falling into one of three functional categories (Modify, Operate, change-Viewport). Procedure: (1) training, a 5-minute video on SketchUp's conventional mouse/keyboard modeling concepts, then 5 minutes of free exploration with the multitouch gestures/menus; (2) testing, each participant completed two "copy this on-screen model" tasks of increasing complexity under both conditions (MT: enlarged toolbar/button multitouch only; MM: toolbars hidden, speech+touch only with a printed command cheat-sheet). Condition order was counterbalanced (half did MT then MM, half the reverse), a within-subjects, counterbalanced design. After both tasks/conditions, participants stated an overall preference and could freely build a model of their choosing with whichever interface they preferred.

**Strengths:**
- Fully within-subjects, counterbalanced design (order randomized across the 12 participants), controls for learning/order effects despite the small N, which a CHI reviewer would credit as sound basic experimental hygiene.
- Triangulates multiple measure types, objective (completion time, touch coordinates/heatmaps, undo-based error counts), subjective/standardized (NASA-TLX), and qualitative preference, rather than relying on a single metric, giving a richer picture than time-on-task alone.
- Transparent and honest about null/weak results and about the study's own limitations: explicitly reports the non-significant ANOVA on completion time, attributes it to small N and outliers, and states outlier causes (large hands, accented speech misrecognition) rather than suppressing them.
- The heatmap visualization of touch coordinates is a nice, low-cost qualitative-quantitative hybrid measure that gives mechanistic insight (visual-attention/hand-shift cost of toolbar navigation) beyond what time/error numbers alone would show.
- Ecologically grounded task design (copy a shown reference model, two escalating complexity levels) plausibly approximates real conceptual-modeling subtasks (drawing, extrusion, navigation) rather than an artificial pointing/selection task.

**Weaknesses:**
- Extremely small sample (N=12) for a comparative quantitative claim, explicitly acknowledged by the authors as the likely reason the primary ANOVA is non-significant, this is a pilot study dressed with inferential statistics it is underpowered to support.
- Error measure is narrow and confounded: 'errors' = number of Undo commands, which conflates true task mistakes with simple exploratory undo use, and is itself mediated by users' differing willingness to use Undo vs. just continuing.
- Multiple comparisons without correction: separate one-tailed t-tests are run per task (Task1, Task2) on error counts in addition to the omnibus ANOVA, inflating Type-I error risk; a CHI reviewer would flag the one-tailed choice too, since it assumes a directional hypothesis (MM<MT errors) decided post hoc.
- No inferential statistics reported for the NASA-TLX subscales or the heatmap comparison, both are presented purely descriptively/graphically, so the paper's rhetorical claims about 'increased frustration and physical demand' in MT are only visually suggestive, not statistically supported.
- Confounds acknowledged but not controlled: one outlier participant's poor performance in MT is attributed to unusually large hands (button-hit-target issue specific to that hardware/UI, not multitouch per se), another's poor MM performance to speech-recognizer accent bias, both threaten construct validity of the comparison but were not excluded, re-run, or statistically adjusted for.
- Preference measure is an unvalidated, ad hoc forced choice + open discussion rather than a validated instrument (the authors themselves flag the missing Likert-based preference measure as future work), limiting the rigor of the 9/12-preferred-MM headline finding.
- Single-session, single-population study (young office workers, presumably at one company/lab site, ages 20-29) with no non-expert-vs-expert or demographic diversity comparison, and no test of retention/learning over multiple sessions, limits generalizability to broader non-expert/community populations.
- Speech vocabulary is tiny and rigid (8 fixed commands), tested only on unambiguous, well-separated command words rather than open-ended natural language, so the 'speech-driven modeling' claim generalizes weakly to freer voice interaction.
- No control for practice effects beyond order counterbalancing, since every participant did both tasks under both conditions, cross-condition transfer of learned modeling strategy is possible and not modeled.

**Sample size / conditions:** N=12 (8 male, 4 female), ages 20 to 29, drawn from an "office environment," described as computer-literate but non-expert at 3D CAD. Two within-subjects conditions (MT vs. MM) × two tasks (T1, T2, increasing complexity), fully crossed with counterbalanced condition order. No control/baseline beyond the two conditions themselves; no separate novice-vs-expert comparison; no long-term/longitudinal component (single session per participant).

**Measures used:** Task completion time (minutes, logged automatically); X-Y touch-point coordinates (used post hoc to build attention/hand-movement heatmaps comparing MT vs. MM); user errors, operationalized narrowly as the number of "Undo" commands issued (a mis-selection proxy); NASA Task Load Index (TLX) administered after each modeling task (subscales: mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, frustration); post-task forced open-ended preference statement/discussion (which interface participants would choose to freely model with, 9/12 chose MM). Stats: two-factor ANOVA with replication (technique × task) on completion times (F=0.468, F_crit=4.062, n.s., α=.05); one-tailed t-tests per task on error counts (Task1: t(11)=1.02, p=.164, n.s.; Task2: t(11)=3.07, p=.005, significant favoring MM). TLX and heatmap results reported only descriptively/graphically, not statistically tested.

**Supplementary material notes:** No separate supplementary/appendix document exists for this paper (it is a 4-page ICMI short paper with all content, figures, and references in the single PDF); there is no additional supplementary material to review beyond the main text obtained (full text retrieved from https://shiftleft.com/mirrors/www.hpl.hp.com/india/documents/papers/icmi4111d-sharma-madhvanath.pdf, an open mirror of the original HP Labs India paper page, ICMI '11, ACM 978-1-4503-0641-6/11/11, DOI 10.1145/2070481.2070538).

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the main related-work section as the direct empirical precedent establishing that combining speech with direct multitouch manipulation lowers physical/attentional workload and errors relative to toolbar-based interaction for non-expert 3D modeling, which grounds our design rationale for a voice-first, button-less interaction layer atop Pascal for non-expert community co-design.

**Cite in supplementary:** Yes, MozArt's own stated limitations (N=12 underpowered for the ANOVA, undo-count as a crude error proxy, open-mic speech false positives in shared/multi-user settings, and the absence of a validated Likert preference instrument) are a useful named methodological precedent for our supplementary materials: they justify why we should (a) recruit a larger sample or treat early pilots as qualitative-only, (b) use a more diagnostic error taxonomy than raw undo counts, (c) budget explicitly for the open-mic/multi-user false-positive problem MozArt flags as unsolved (highly relevant since ours is explicitly multi-user), and (d) include a validated preference/usability scale (e.g., SUS) rather than ad hoc forced choice.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "While a larger study is required to obtain statistically significant comparison regarding efficiency and accuracy of the two interfaces, a majority of the participants preferred the multimodal interface over the multitouch." (Abstract)
- "We compared task completion times using a two factor ANOVA test with replication alpha=0.05, which yielded F=0.468 < Fcritical=4.062, p>0.05. This shows that the influence of the technique on the task completion time is not significant."
- "For Task2 a one-tailed t-test found (t(11)=3.07, p=0.005). Thus for Task 2 the MM condition produced significantly fewer errors than the MT condition, whereas for Task1 there was no significant difference."
- "...we did observe that some of the task completion times were unusually large. For example, one of the participants with unusually large hands had issues selecting the correct menu buttons in the MT condition. Another user had an accent that was not recognized well by the speech recognizer.", the authors flag confounds/outliers themselves rather than hiding them.
- "On completion of both tasks, all participants were given an opportunity to select one of the two interfaces and build a model of their own choice; 9 out of 12 participants chose the MM interface."
- "We conducted a Two Factor ANOVA with replication and found the results to be statistically insignificant, primarily due to the small number of participants taken for the study." (authors' own limitation statement)
- "Speech input using an open microphone suffers from the problem of false positives (spurious input) due to ambient sounds, especially in multi-user environments. This could be solved by adding lip movement detection...to detect when the user is speaking.", directly relevant precedent for our multi-user, voice-first co-design context.
- "We would also like to include a Likert test to study subjective preference for the two techniques" (acknowledged gap: no validated preference/usability instrument was used, only ad hoc open discussion).

### Digital Modeling for Everyone: Exploring How Novices Approach Voice-Based 3D Modeling
- **Authors:** Giuseppe Desolda, Andrea Esposito, Florian Müller, Sebastian Feger
- **Venue/Year:** INTERACT 2023 (Springer LNCS) 2023
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.04481
- **Relevance:** Wizard-of-Oz study (n=22) of how non-expert novices form mental models when using voice alone to shape and select 3D objects; directly informs command vocabulary and error-handling design for voice-first, non-expert 3D co-design with community groups.

**Methodology summary:** A high-fidelity Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) qualitative study addressing RQ: "How does the mental model of novices translate into voice-based 3D modeling?" 22 novice participants (no 3D-modeling experience) each completed 14 graphically-presented tasks (object creation, manipulation, scale/rotate/move, and composite-object construction, culminating in building a chair from primitives) by speaking voice commands to a system they believed was a real voice-controlled Blender assistant. In reality, a hidden human "Wizard" (one of four researchers, rotated to reduce bias) manually executed each spoken command via Blender's Python API in real time, with OBS-driven UI overlays (listening/processing/result states) sustaining the illusion. Sessions ran remotely over Zoom (~30 min each), followed by a demographics + open-ended + task-understandability questionnaire. Data (session transcripts + questionnaire responses) were analyzed with inductive Thematic Analysis: two independent researcher pairs (one per language group) open-coded, merged codes into higher-level groups, extracted themes, then reconciled across pairs in a joint session, yielding 5 themes (Basic Operations, Selection of Objects, Errors, The Gulf of Execution, Users' Requests) and a corresponding set of design implications for voice-based 3D modeling tools.

**Strengths:**
- Genuine novice-only sampling (self-rated 3D knowledge 1.6/10) directly targets the population most prior CAD/voice-CAD studies excluded (prior work sampled 5 to 41 skilled/expert users), making it one of the only studies eliciting a truly novice mental model rather than an expert one.
- High-fidelity WoZ execution (live Python/Blender API scripting plus OBS-driven listening/processing UI states) is a methodologically strong illusion-maintenance design that plausibly avoided tipping participants off, increasing ecological validity of the elicited commands.
- Rigorous, transparent thematic-analysis process: two independent researcher pairs coded each language subsample separately, then reconciled codes/themes in a joint session, a form of investigator triangulation that strengthens credibility of the qualitative findings beyond a single coder's interpretation.
- Deliberate mitigation of a specific, named bias (the 'primer effect' of textual task descriptions) by using graphical+diagram task prompts instead of verbal descriptions, with pilot-testing (n=3, separate from main sample) and post-hoc validation of task understandability (8.2/10).
- Findings translate into specific, actionable, mechanism-level design implications (e.g., two concrete alternative repair strategies for vague commands, three axis-reference conventions to support, chained-command handling) rather than vague high-level takeaways, making it unusually easy to operationalize for a real system.
- Rich, well-documented apparatus and procedure (dual-language wizard/conductor teams with role rotation to reduce individual-researcher bias, standardized introductory script across languages) supports replicability.

**Weaknesses:**
- No comparison/control condition: the study is single-condition (voice-only WoZ for everyone), so no baseline against GUI, multimodal, or a non-WoZ real system exists within the paper to isolate what is specific to voice versus general to novice 3D reasoning, a CHI reviewer would flag the lack of any contrast condition as limiting causal claims about voice interaction per se.
- Small, non-representative, convenience sample (n=22, recruited from authors' personal social circles) with narrow demographics (mean age 24, majority female, only two nationalities, no reported ethnicity/SES diversity), the authors themselves flag this, but a reviewer would still note limited generalizability beyond young, educated, WEIRD-adjacent participants.
- Study conducted in English for all participants despite it being a non-native language for the entire sample, which the authors admit may have shaped command phrasing/vocabulary independent of any voice-interaction effect, confounding linguistic performance with mental-model elicitation.
- Task complexity ceiling is low (culminating in a chair made of 2 boxes + 4 cylinders); real digital-fabrication objects are typically far more geometrically complex, and operations like texturing, hole-making, or non-primitive freeform shapes are entirely untested, limiting how far the 'lessons learned' generalize to realistic co-design tasks.
- Apparatus confound: Blender's WIMP-style visual interface (buttons, menus, panels) was still visible in the background during the WoZ sessions, which the authors acknowledge may have biased participants toward WIMP-influenced mental models rather than a 'clean slate' voice-native mental model, a purpose-built voice-only interface might have produced different themes.
- No inter-rater reliability statistic (e.g., Cohen's kappa) reported for the thematic analysis despite involving four coders across two independent pairs, reconciliation was described narratively ('minor differences... identical in meaning') rather than quantified, which a quantitatively-minded CHI reviewer might push back on even though qualitative purists would consider this unnecessary for reflexive TA.
- No quantitative outcome measures (task success/completion time/error rate) are reported despite a controlled task structure that could have supported them, leaving 'how well' novices could actually accomplish tasks via voice unaddressed, only qualitative characterization of strategies and errors is provided, so effectiveness/efficiency claims cannot be made.

**Sample size / conditions:** N=22 (15 female, 7 male), convenience-sampled from the authors' social circles; 11 Italian and 11 German, mean age 24.1 (SD=3.7, range 21 to 34). All had essentially no 3D-modeling experience (self-rated 3D knowledge 1.6/10, SD=1.1); moderate general IT knowledge (6.5/10) and low-to-moderate voice-assistant familiarity (3.1/10). Education: ~45% high-school-level, ~36% Bachelor's, ~14% Master's. 15/22 lacked STEM education. Single-condition, within-subjects design (no comparison condition, e.g., no GUI-only or multimodal control group), every participant did all 14 tasks under the same WoZ voice-only setup. Study run entirely in English (non-native for all participants) to avoid language-specific confounds. A separate 3-participant pilot (excluded from the final n=22) was used only to validate the graphical task representations.

**Measures used:** Primary data: ~11 hours of transcribed WoZ session audio/video, annotated with pauses, WoZ processing start/end, and error/over-correction markers. Post-session questionnaire captured: (1) demographics; (2) three open-ended questions on likes/dislikes about the system and willingness to adopt it (motivated); (3) a 1 to 10 task-understandability rating for the graphical task format (used only to validate the graphical-task methodology, not as an outcome measure of the voice interface itself). No standardized usability instrument (e.g., SUS, NASA-TLX) was used; no quantitative performance metrics (task completion time, success rate, error rate) were reported as primary outcomes, the core analytic method was inductive Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) applied to the qualitative corpus, run independently by two researcher pairs (splitting Italian/German data) and reconciled via joint coding/theme comparison and naming session.

**Supplementary material notes:** The paper explicitly references an "attached appendix"/supplementary materials bundle (referred to inconsistently as being both an arXiv-attached appendix and a "supplementary materials" folder) containing: the full task table and graphical task images (sub-folder "tasks"), the introductory script used with participants (sub-folder "introductory script"), the questionnaire instrument (sub-folder "questionnaire"), the final codes and themes with their relationships (sub-folder "Codes and Themes"), and a Python module of pre-defined Blender API functions used by the Wizard (sub-folder "python module"). This is a strong precedent worth citing in our own supplementary materials for how to document a WoZ study's codebook, task materials, and wizard-support tooling transparently; I was not able to independently browse/verify the live contents of this appendix beyond the text's own descriptions of it (no separate appendix URL was fetched).

**Cite in main paper:** Cite in the main paper's related-work as the only prior study eliciting non-expert *novices'* (not domain experts') mental models for voice-only 3D shape creation/selection via a controlled Wizard-of-Oz design, directly motivating our voice-first command vocabulary and error-handling requirements for non-expert community co-design.

**Cite in supplementary:** The two concrete self-repair mechanisms the authors propose for vague/incomplete voice commands, context-informed "sensible defaults" (with a confirm-the-default hybrid) versus "interactively guiding the user" for missing parameters, are a direct, reusable methodological/design precedent for our supplementary materials' description of how our system's NLU layer should handle ambiguous community-member commands during collaborative 3D specification.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "Understand user corrections and adapt to them", recognizing uncertainty, hesitation, doubt and error awareness early on is particularly crucial in digital modeling contexts, since users disclose them frequently due to unfamiliarity with 3D modeling.
- "Deal with vague and incomplete commands" via two concrete mechanisms: (1) sensible defaults informed by dialog context (e.g., cylinder diameter defaults to the side of the cube it sits on) and (2) interactively asking the user for missing parameters; a hybrid confirm-the-default approach is proposed.
- Around 45% of participants in the final composite-object task (building a chair) spontaneously invoked an 'implicit construction shape' (e.g., 'place the four cylinders at the four corners of a square') that did not exist in the scene, asking the system to create it, use it as a reference, and delete it afterward.
- Novices select objects via at least 7 distinct strategies (absolute by name/shape/2D-approximation, relative by creation-order/position/dimension/inversion-of-selection, and implicit last-edited-object reference) and frequently mix strategies within a single command, the paper argues selection is 'among the most critical' operations to support.
- Three axis-referencing conventions coexist unprompted: relative direction ('to the right'), color ('the green axis'), and formal name ('the X axis'); several participants also expected Y (not Z) to be the vertical axis, mirroring 2D-drawing convention.
- Limitation acknowledged by the authors: Blender's WIMP-oriented UI (commands, buttons, functions) may have biased or confused novices despite hiding unused UI, and the tool itself was not purpose-built for voice interaction, a purpose-built system might elicit a different mental model.
- Limitation: tasks capped at chair-level composite complexity and did not cover texturing, holes, or other common fabrication operations; sample was two nationalities (11 Italian, 11 German) with study conducted in English (non-native for all), leaving language, ethnicity, socio-economic status, and age effects on mental model unexamined.
- Task materials were graphical (image + brief prompt) rather than textual, explicitly to avoid the 'primer effect' biasing command wording, validated via 3-participant pilot and a post-hoc understandability rating (mean/median 8.2/10, SD 1.0), though task 11 still required conductor intervention (webcam demonstration) for 4 participants.

### Gesture and Speech Elicitation for 3D CAD Modeling in Conceptual Design
- **Authors:** Sumbul Khan, Bige Tunçer
- **Venue/Year:** Automation in Construction 2019
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0926580517306106
- **Relevance:** Elicitation study (n=41 architects/engineers) producing a vocabulary of speech commands and gestures for 3D CAD primitives, manipulation, and navigation; useful architecture-domain grounding for designing voice commands for zoning-compliant building co-design.

**Methodology summary:** This is a classic Wobbrock-style user elicitation study (explicitly building on Wobbrock, Morris &amp; Wilson 2009's "user-defined gestures" paradigm) adapted to the specialized domain of architectural/engineering CAD modeling and extended to multimodal (speech+gesture) elicitation. Participants were shown a referent (image/video clip of a CAD action or object) and asked to produce, unprompted, the gesture (Session A) or gesture+speech (Session B) they would naturally use to invoke it, order counterbalanced across the two sessions to control for order effects. Elicited gestures were video-recorded from two angles and then subjected to qualitative thematic coding by three independent coders to extract recurring morphological "gesture families" per referent, alongside quantitative tabulation of speech-term frequencies (agreement scores) per referent. The compiled, most-frequent gesture/speech pairs were then subjected to a second-pass expert evaluation, in which experts rated the suitability of each compiled gesture for its CAD referent, an added validation layer beyond raw participant frequency that most elicitation studies skip. The output is a design-recommendation vocabulary (not a working system), later partially implemented and pilot-tested in the companion GesCAD paper (Kinect+Rhino plugin, n=6 usability test with Likert ratings and task timing, explicitly flagged by the authors as too small for valid Likert-scale inference).

**Strengths:**
- Sample (n=41) is larger and more domain-specific than most gesture-elicitation studies, with a deliberately balanced split between architecture (49%) and engineering (51%) professionals and near-even gender split, appropriate rigor for grounding a specialized-domain vocabulary.
- Two-condition, order-counterbalanced within-subjects design (gesture-only vs. gesture+speech) actually tests a substantive question, whether adding speech changes gesture production, rather than just cataloguing a single modality.
- Coding rigor: three independent coders, explicit citation of an intercoder-reliability/content-analysis standard (Lombard et al. 2002), and a two-stage funnel (raw thematic coding → compiled candidate set → independent expert suitability re-rating) that goes beyond the bare 'agreement score' typical of Wobbrock-paradigm papers.
- Directly and explicitly positions itself against 'arbitrary vocabularies... employed in extant literature,' i.e. the paper's whole raison d'être is the same argument our project needs to make for its own voice-command design (empirically elicited > researcher-invented commands).
- Mixed-methods reporting: qualitative morphological themes plus quantitative frequency/agreement tables plus Likert-based expert validation, giving multiple convergent lines of evidence for the final recommended vocabulary.
- Authors are self-aware about limitations and cite the relevant legacy-bias literature (Morris et al. 2014; Köpsel & Bubalo 2015) rather than ignoring it, and the companion paper explicitly flags its own small-n Likert validity concerns rather than overclaiming.

**Weaknesses:**
- Elicitation stimuli were images and short video clips demonstrating the CAD action/object itself, this risks legacy/demonstration bias (participants may partly mimic what they just saw rather than freely generate a novel gesture), a known confound the elicitation-study literature (which this paper cites) treats seriously; it's not clear from available material that this was controlled for beyond counterbalancing session order.
- No inferential statistics are reported for the core Session-A-vs-Session-B comparison (gesture-only vs. gesture+speech), findings are descriptive (percentages, means) rather than hypothesis-tested, a common but reviewer-flaggable gap for a CHI-caliber elicitation study.
- Participants were domain experts (trained architects/engineers), not lay/non-expert users, the opposite end of the expertise spectrum from our target population (non-expert community members co-designing a building). The elicited vocabulary is expert-legacy-biased toward CAD-literate mental models (e.g. 'extrude,' 'cuboid') that may not transfer to first-time, non-technical users.
- The interaction paradigm elicited is single-user, individual (one participant alone facing a screen), it says nothing about turn-taking, deixis, or vocabulary collisions when multiple co-present people gesture/speak simultaneously at a shared 3D scene, which is the exact multi-user condition our tool operates in.
- Referent vocabulary is limited to atomic CAD primitives and manipulations (move/rotate/scale/zoom/box/cone), it does not address higher-level, domain-specific referents relevant to zoning-compliant co-design (e.g., setbacks, envelope constraints, unit counts, FAR), so the reusable vocabulary itself is narrow; only the elicitation methodology transfers cleanly.
- The companion GesCAD system-evaluation study (same research group, same elicited vocabulary put into practice) used only n=6, and the authors themselves state the Likert-scale results 'have limited validity' at that sample size, a caution relevant if we cite this line of work as evidence the vocabulary 'works' in a live system rather than just as elicited preferences.

**Sample size / conditions:** n=41 participants (52% female / 48% male), 49% with architecture backgrounds and 51% with engineering backgrounds, recruited at Singapore University of Technology and Design (SUTD) over a 2-week period. Within-subjects design with two counterbalanced sessions: Session A = gesture-only articulation; Session B = gesture and/or speech articulation. Participants sat ~10 ft from a screen and were shown images and short video clips depicting 12 CAD-modeling "referents" across three categories: primitives (e.g., box, cone, prism, freeform), manipulations (move, rotate, scale, copy), and navigation (zoom, pan, orbit). Two video cameras (different angles) recorded gesture production; speech was transcribed. A companion/follow-on paper (GesCAD, OzCHI 2017) then ran a separate, much smaller (n=6) usability evaluation of a Kinect+Rhino prototype built from a subset of the elicited vocabulary.

**Measures used:** (1) Gesture morphology: video-coded by three coders into morphological "themes" per referent (e.g., for Zoom-in: push-hands-toward-screen / pull-hands-toward-self / pull-hands-apart), following an explicit intercoder-reliability procedure (citing Lombard, Snyder-Duch &amp; Bracken 2002 content-analysis reliability standards). (2) Speech command vocabulary: frequency/agreement percentages of the specific words participants used per referent (Wobbrock-style agreement scoring), e.g. for "Move": "Move" 70.73%, "Slide" 12.20%, "Push" 7.32%, "Shift" 7.32%, "Translate" 2.43%. Total of 984 gestures logged across both sessions. (3) Gesture-type classification: unimanual vs. bimanual-symmetrical vs. bimanual-action-reference. (4) Post-hoc expert suitability ratings (Likert-type) of the compiled/final gesture set by category: bimanual action-reference M=3.61, unimanual M=2.90, bimanual symmetrical M=2.53. In the companion GesCAD usability study: 5-point Likert ratings of ease-of-task, satisfaction-with-result, fun, memorability, physical comfort, creative-exploration potential, plus task completion times.

**Supplementary material notes:** No separate supplementary materials/appendix file for the 2019 Automation in Construction paper could be located publicly (paywalled on ScienceDirect; only abstract/outline/reference list accessible via Academia.edu mirror). However, the paper's own outline (Introduction, Related Work, Method, Participants, Experiment Design, Data Analysis: Coding, Results from User Experiment, Thematic Analysis of Elicited Gestures, Qualitative Findings, Discussion, Limitations and Further Work) plus two companion papers by the same authors, Khan & Tunçer, "User Defined Conceptual Modeling Gestures" (2018, reports the same n=41 study with full gesture-type breakdowns) and Khan, Rajapakse, Zhang, Nanayakkara, Tunçer & Blessing, "GesCAD: An Intuitive Interface for Conceptual Architectural Design" (OzCHI 2017, full text obtained and read in full: https://haimoz.github.io/assets/papers/khan2017gescad.pdf), together substitute for an appendix and were used to reconstruct full methodological detail here.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the main related-work section, in one sentence, as the direct empirical precedent for the core methodological claim that voice/gesture commands for 3D spatial design must be user-elicited from the target population rather than arbitrarily designed by system builders, which is exactly the argument we make (and must extend to non-expert, multi-user community co-designers) for our own voice-command vocabulary.

**Cite in supplementary:** Yes, two concrete methodological details belong in supplementary as precedent/caution when we describe our own voice/gesture elicitation (if performed): (1) their use of demonstration video-clip stimuli as elicitation referents, which risks legacy/mimicry bias, a design choice we should explicitly avoid or control for when eliciting commands from non-expert community members; and (2) their companion GesCAD evaluation's self-flagged limitation that Likert-scale results at n=6 'have limited validity,' which is a directly relevant precedent for calibrating our own sample-size claims if we run a small pilot usability evaluation of our voice interface.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "We conducted an experiment with 41 participants from architecture and engineering backgrounds to elicit preferences of gestures and speech for 3D CAD modeling referents such as primitives, manipulations, and navigation." (abstract)
- "Bimanual action reference gestures were favored, showing advantages in spatial and manipulative tasks", expert ratings: bimanual action-reference M=3.61 vs. bimanual symmetrical M=2.53 vs. unimanual M=2.90.
- "A total of 984 gestures were logged across both elicitation sessions."
- Move vocabulary example: "Move" (70.73%), "Slide" (12.20%), "Push" (7.32%), "Shift" (7.32%), "Translate" (2.43%).
- From the GesCAD companion paper's self-critique (n=6 evaluation): "We acknowledge that the use of Likert scale has limited validity in case of small sample sizes... findings can only be considered indicative."
- GesCAD paper also flags that referents shown to participants as demo video clips during training led participants to "freely improvise gestures they had seen in the demo" rather than consult a written manual, a legacy-bias-adjacent observation about stimulus presentation shaping elicited behavior.

### LLMR: Real-time Prompting of Interactive Worlds using Large Language Models
- **Authors:** Fernanda De La Torre, Cathy Mengying Fang, Han Huang, Andrzej Banburski-Fahey, Judith Amores Fernandez, Jaron Lanier
- **Venue/Year:** CHI 2024 2024
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642579
- **Relevance:** LLM-driven framework for real-time natural-language creation and editing of interactive 3D worlds (scene understanding, task planning, self-debugging); a strong precedent for the LLM-agent scene-editing layer needed to translate voice/NL requests into edits on the Pascal 3D scene.

**Methodology summary:** LLMR reports two distinct evaluations bundled into one paper. (1) A benchmark/numerical study of the system's core LLM pipeline (Planner → Scene Analyzer → Skill Library retrieval → Builder → Inspector, i.e., a multi-agent decomposition with self-debugging loops) run over two curated sets of ~150 natural-language prompts each, one set targeting scene creation from an empty world, one targeting modification/editing of an existing populated scene, spanning task types from single-object placement to multi-step sequential instructions, plus integration of external assets (Sketchfab, DALL-E generated textures). Success/error is scored via the Inspector module's automated validation (compile-time + runtime checks) rather than exclusively human judgment, and the paper reports error-rate reduction (~4x lower than vanilla GPT-4) broken out by difficulty level, plus completion-time numbers (~1 minute per task on average) and ablations isolating the contribution of each pipeline component (Planner, Scene Analyzer, Builder-Inspector self-debug loop, Skill Library). (2) A small usability study, N=11 participants with mixed prior Unity/VR experience (expert vs. non-expert), who used the natural-language interface to iteratively create/edit a 3D scene; data collected via a custom Likert-scale questionnaire (full items in Appendix D, not visible in the fetched excerpt) plus semi-structured interviews covering prompting strategy, comparison to traditional 3D workflows, and user expectations/surprises. Analysis was qualitative thematic coding of interview transcripts supplemented by descriptive statistics on the questionnaire; no inferential statistics, no control condition, no comparison against a non-LLM or alternative-LLM baseline in the usability study itself.

**Strengths:**
- Dual-track evaluation is a reasonable design for an agentic system paper: an automated, scalable benchmark (300 prompts total) for the core technical claim (error-rate reduction) decoupled from a smaller human-centered usability check, this separation of concerns (system performance vs. user experience) is something a CHI reviewer would likely praise as appropriately scoped for a systems/HCI hybrid contribution.
- Ablation study isolating each pipeline module's (Planner, Scene Analyzer, Builder-Inspector, Skill Library) contribution to error reduction gives a credible mechanistic account of why the system works, not just that it works, stronger than a black-box 'ours beats baseline' claim.
- Explicit, self-critical discussion of failure modes (Inspector leniency on repeated passes, stochastic unpredictability, unsuitability for precision-critical tasks) rather than only touting success, a hallmark reviewers reward.
- Task-difficulty stratification of error rate (rather than one aggregate number) allows readers to see where the system degrades, which is useful precedent for reporting granular performance rather than a single headline metric.

**Weaknesses:**
- Usability N=11 is small and, worse, run as a single within-subjects exposure with no baseline/control tool (no comparison to a non-LLM VR authoring workflow or to a competing LLM 3D tool), a CHI reviewer would flag this as unable to support comparative claims of 'better' UX, only descriptive plausibility.
- No standardized, validated instrument (no SUS, no NASA-TLX, no validated creativity-support index), reliance on a custom, unvalidated Likert questionnaire (Appendix D) weakens comparability to other systems and to future replications.
- Error-rate ground truth for the 300-prompt benchmark is determined by the system's own Inspector/automated validation rather than independent human/expert judges with inter-rater reliability reported, this is a circularity risk a reviewer would likely probe, especially given the paper's own admission that the Inspector can become lenient on repeated passes.
- No description (in the retrievable text) of who authored/curated the 150+150 prompts, whether they were piloted, or whether they reflect realistic end-user phrasing versus researcher-generated prompts, a threat to ecological validity for claims about real-world natural-language robustness.
- No reported IRB approval statement or participant compensation/recruitment method in the fetched text, and no demographic breakdown beyond 'diverse' and 'varying Unity experience', thin reporting on human-subjects rigor by CHI standards.
- Purely qualitative/descriptive analysis of the usability data (no inferential statistics, no effect sizes, no pre-registered hypotheses) means claims like 'participants found it intuitive' rest on small-N self-report and researcher-coded interview themes, susceptible to confirmation bias.
- Single completion-time metric (~1 minute) is reported without variance/distribution, and it is unclear whether this includes LLM API latency variability, which matters for a voice-first real-time interaction claim like the one in our own project.

**Sample size / conditions:** Two evaluation tracks: (a) benchmark track, two sets of ~150 natural-language prompts each (empty-scene creation vs. existing-scene modification), evaluated automatically for code error rate against a standalone GPT-4 baseline and via internal ablations of pipeline components, no human participants; (b) usability track, N=11 participants, described only as "diverse" with varying Unity/VR experience (expert/non-expert split noted but no demographic table visible), single within-subjects exposure to the system (no A/B or baseline-tool comparison condition), no control group, no reported IRB statement in the fetched text.

**Measures used:** Benchmark track: automated error rate (compile + runtime validation via the Inspector module), error rate stratified by task difficulty level, task completion time, ablation deltas per pipeline component. Usability track: custom Likert-scale questionnaire (full item wording in Appendix D, not retrievable from the fetched HTML excerpt), semi-structured interview protocol (prompting strategy, comparison to conventional 3D-authoring workflows, expectations/surprises), qualitative thematic coding of interview responses, descriptive stats on questionnaire responses. No standardized instruments (no SUS, no NASA-TLX) were identified as used.

**Supplementary material notes:** Full item wording for the usability questionnaire and interview protocol is in the paper's Appendix D, referenced as containing the "User study Questionnaire and Interview Questions," but the exact Likert item text was not retrievable from the fetched arXiv HTML rendering in this session (content truncated/not rendered in the accessible excerpt); recommend a follow-up direct PDF read (arXiv 2309.12276, Appendix D) if the exact item wording is needed verbatim for our supplementary comparison table.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the main paper's related work: LLMR is the closest existing technical precedent for exactly the component our project needs, an LLM-agent pipeline that translates real-time natural-language input into validated edits on a live 3D scene (scene understanding, task planning, code generation, self-debugging), so it grounds the feasibility of our voice-to-Pascal-scene-edit layer while our contribution is clearly differentiated as applying that class of system to multi-user, non-expert, zoning-compliant civic co-design rather than single-user VR content creation.

**Cite in supplementary:** Also worth a methodological-precedent note in supplementary materials: LLMR's own reported failure mode, the self-debugging/Inspector module becoming lenient on repeated validation passes, i.e., an automated code-checker's reliability degrading under iteration, is a concrete, citable methodological hazard for us to name and guard against in our supplementary description of how we'll validate AI-proposed edits for zoning compliance (we should not rely solely on an LLM-based inspector without independent/human-in-the-loop or rule-based compliance checks), and their small-N (11), single-condition, non-validated-instrument usability study is a useful cautionary comparison point for justifying a more rigorous (larger N, validated measures, or comparative condition) study design in our own supplementary methods.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- "a usability study (N=11) with a diverse set that revealed participants had positive experiences with the system and would use it again"
- "LLMR outperforms the standard GPT-4 by 4x in average error rate" across both empty-scene and existing-scene test sets
- "Participants found LLMR to be intuitive and easy to use, and they were able to iteratively achieve desired outputs without much manual scripting"
- Authors explicitly acknowledge "unpredictability due to generative models' stochastic nature" and that the tool is not suited for contexts "that require precise and specific control"
- Noted failure mode: the Inspector module shows "increased leniency in repeated inspections," letting suboptimal/buggy code pass self-debugging checks
- Framework depends on external third-party APIs (OpenAI, Sketchfab, DALL-E), introducing dependency/availability risk not deeply discussed as a validity threat

### Designing Conversational Multimodal 3D Printed Models with People who are Blind
- **Authors:** Samuel Reinders, Swamy Ananthanarayan, Matthew Butler, Kim Marriott
- **Venue/Year:** DIS 2023 (ACM Designing Interactive Systems) 2023
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3563657.3595989
- **Relevance:** Co-design of conversational, multimodal (voice + touch) 3D artifacts with blind participants; supports the paper's accessibility/legitimacy argument that voice-first 3D co-design can include non-expert and disabled community members typically excluded from CAD tools.

**Methodology summary:** Two-phase, mixed-methods study. Phase 1 (co-design): four 90-minute sessions (S1-S4) iteratively built "Solar I3M," a tangible solar-system model combining touch gestures, haptic vibration, and a Dialogflow conversational interface. S1 and S2 were group workshops with 4 novice co-designers each (S1: touch gestures; S2: audio labelling, conversational interface, haptic vibration). S3 and S4 were one-on-one sessions with a single "expert" co-designer who had prior (unrelated) design experience, revisiting touch/conversational/haptic topics in more depth. Design changes (e.g., touch-gesture timing thresholds, wake-word choice, voice type/speed/verbosity options, demonstrative-pronoun support, haptic-vibration timing) were made live during sessions based on co-designer reactions, then carried forward. Phase 2 (evaluation): a single ~90-minute usability session per participant with 7 new blind participants (recruited from the same lab pool, none overlapping with co-design participants). Each session had: (a) 20-25 min hands-on training (tactile-only exploration first, then gesture and voice-command training), (b) 20 min customisation walkthrough (voice type: feminine/masculine/robotic; speed: 100/150/200%; response length: shorter/longer), (c) a 10-15 min activity split between-subjects for time reasons: 3 participants did 5 short structured information-retrieval tasks, 4 did unstructured free exploration. Data collection: a modified System Usability Scale (wording adapted with input from the S3/S4 co-designer for blind-accessibility relevance) plus a 30-minute semi-structured interview on touch/conversational/multimodal experience and sense of agency, independence, and expectations. All sessions were video-recorded and transcribed. Analysis: thematic analysis combining in-session comments, researcher observations, and interview transcripts. An initial codebook was seeded from co-design-phase observations; three researchers independently coded one evaluation session, met to extend/reconcile codes, then all sessions were coded with conflicts resolved in a follow-up meeting, and themes were iteratively refined and consolidated into four cross-cutting themes: prior experience with personal technology, independence & control, confidence & comfort, and customisation & personalisation.

**Strengths:**
- Genuine, iterative co-design (not just consultation): design parameters (gesture timing, wake word, voice, verbosity, haptic timing) were changed live in-session based on direct co-designer feedback and reactions, giving the design decisions strong end-user provenance.
- Reflexive, transparent coding process: initial codebook seeded from earlier co-design observations, 3 independent coders, explicit reconciliation meeting for conflicts, a defensible qualitative rigor process CHI/DIS reviewers respect.
- Thoughtful accessibility-specific instrument adaptation: SUS wording was modified in consultation with a blind co-designer rather than applied off-the-shelf, addressing a common criticism of quantitative usability instruments used uncritically with disabled populations.
- Rich, well-triangulated qualitative data: combines observation, interview, and in-situ think-aloud-style comments, with many concrete verbatim quotes tied to specific participants (P1-P7), improving traceability/credibility.
- Explicit and honest limitations section (single artifact, small N, no low-vision comparison) rather than overclaiming generalizability.
- Novel contribution clearly scoped: first I3M with a fully integrated (not smartphone-relayed) conversational interface plus demonstrative-pronoun support, and the paper is explicit about what is genuinely new versus prior work.

**Weaknesses:**
- Very small, single-site, convenience samples (9 co-designers across 4 uneven sessions; 7 evaluation participants) recruited from a single lab's existing contact pool, limits generalisability and risks including participants already comfortable with lab-style user studies and voice assistants (all 7 evaluation participants were already regular conversational-interface users, a likely non-representative skew).
- No baseline/control condition: the evaluation has no comparison to a unimodal I3M, a touch-only I3M, or an existing accessible-graphics tool, so claims that the multimodal/conversational design specifically drove the high SUS score and positive themes are not causally isolated, could partly reflect novelty effect or general researcher-presence enthusiasm (Hawthorne-type effect) in a single ~90-min session.
- The structured (n=3) vs. unstructured (n=4) task split is described as a between-subjects comparison but was adopted purely for time constraints, not randomized or powered, the paper's claim of 'very minor differences' between groups rests on an uncontrolled, likely underpowered contrast.
- Session count and co-designer counts across S1-S4 are somewhat asymmetric (two 4-person workshops vs. two 1-person expert sessions), and it's unclear how much the single 'expert' co-designer's prior design experience (atypical of the target population) skewed S3/S4 outcomes relative to the naive co-designers in S1/S2.
- No inter-rater reliability statistic (e.g., Cohen's kappa) reported for the thematic coding despite three independent coders, reconciliation is described narratively but not quantified, which some reviewers would want for a stated 'independent coding' claim.
- All sessions were single-exposure (~90 minutes total including training) with no longitudinal or repeat-use component, so findings about 'confidence' and 'comfort' developing over the session cannot speak to real, sustained adoption or long-term customisation behavior.
- SUS was 'modified' for the blind-user context but the specific item wording changes and their validity/comparability to the standard SUS are not psychometrically evaluated, this makes the 84.29 score somewhat incomparable to standard SUS benchmarks.

**Sample size / conditions:** Co-design phase: 9 blind co-designers total (4 in S1, 4 in S2, could be same or different from S1's 4, paper implies "different groups" so likely 8 distinct plus 1 recurring expert co-designer across S3/S4 = 9 total unique co-designers per abstract). Evaluation phase: 7 new blind participants (ages mid-20s to mid-70s, all self-identified blind, some with minimal light perception; 6/7 could read braille; all used at least one conversational interface regularly). No control condition or comparison system (e.g., no comparison against a non-multimodal or non-conversational I3M baseline), this is a single-condition, within-subjects usability walkthrough with a between-subjects task-type split (structured n=3 vs. unstructured n=4) used only to compare interaction strategies, not as a formal experimental manipulation.

**Measures used:** (1) Modified System Usability Scale (10 items, adapted wording, reported as per-item means and an overall average SUS score of 84.29); (2) 30-minute semi-structured interview transcripts; (3) researcher observations of interaction behavior during training/customisation/activity; (4) qualitative thematic coding of all the above by 3 independent coders with reconciliation. No behavioral/performance metrics (task completion time, error rate, number of gesture failures) were systematically quantified beyond narrative/observational description.

**Supplementary material notes:** The arXiv preprint (2306.06811) itself contains what would be Appendix Table 6 (full SUS item wordings and per-item response distributions) inline in the body/near end of the paper; no separate appendix or supplementary materials file, video figure, or dataset repository was found via ACM DL, Monash's institutional repository, or Semantic Scholar, the paper appears to have no separate supplementary materials package beyond the in-text tables/figures. (Could not access the ACM DL page directly due to a 403; details reconstructed from the author-hosted arXiv version, which the DBLP/Semantic Scholar record confirms is the same paper, DOI 10.1145/3563657.3595989.)

**Cite in main paper:** Cite in the MAIN paper's related-work/accessibility-legitimacy argument: it is the closest existing precedent for genuine end-user co-design (not just consultation) of a voice-plus-touch multimodal interface with a population (blind users) typically excluded from spatial/CAD design tools, directly supporting the claim that voice-first, multimodal co-design can meaningfully include non-expert and disabled participants in producing a real artifact.

**Cite in supplementary:** Include as a methodological precedent in supplementary materials, specifically: (1) their practice of adapting SUS wording in consultation with a disabled co-designer rather than using it unmodified, a concrete, citable precedent if our study also adapts standard usability/UX measures for voice-first 3D co-design with non-expert or disabled community members; and (2) their self-identified limitation that design recommendations were distilled from a single artifact/domain (solar system model) with a small, single-site sample and no baseline condition, worth citing as a known limitation pattern in participatory 3D-interaction studies that our own supplementary limitations discussion should proactively address (e.g., by testing across multiple building/zoning scenarios rather than one, and by disclosing the lack of a baseline against non-voice-first CAD tools).

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Average SUS score of 84.29 across the 7 evaluation participants, generally very positive.
- Four cross-cutting themes: use of prior experience with personal technology (smartphones/smart speakers), desire for independence & control, confidence & comfort, and desire for customisation & personalisation.
- Participants strongly wanted an 'Activate Press' gesture to prevent accidental triggering during tactile exploration, and wanted haptic vibration to confirm successful gesture activation.
- Demonstrative pronouns ('this', 'these', 'it') were found to make conversational interaction feel more natural and increased willingness to physically manipulate/compare objects.
- One participant (P4) explicitly refused to use conversational interaction in public spaces, preferring touch, contradicts assumption that voice is uniformly preferable for accessibility.
- Google Knowledge Graph fallback search ('I don't know the answer... would you like me to search for it?') increased participant trust/confidence because the system admitted its own limits.
- Customisation needs varied by ability (hearing/memory impairment), personal preference, environment (noisy vs. quiet, public vs. private), content type, and task purpose, a strong argument for adjustable, not one-size-fits-all, voice/haptic/touch parameters.
- Authors explicitly flag as a limitation that recommendations derive from a single I3M (Solar System model) and a small, self-selected sample, and call for replication with larger and low-vision samples.

### A11yShape: AI-Assisted 3-D Modeling for Blind and Low-Vision Programmers
- **Authors:** Zhuohao Zhang, Gene S-H Kim, et al. (Anhong Guo group, University of Michigan, with Univ. of Texas at Dallas, Univ. of Washington, Purdue)
- **Venue/Year:** ASSETS 2025 (27th ACM SIGACCESS Conference on Computers and Accessibility) 2025
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3663547.3746362
- **Relevance:** AI/LLM assistant that gives accessible natural-language descriptions of and edits to parametric 3-D models for blind/low-vision users; recent, directly relevant precedent for pairing an LLM agent with a 3D modeling backend to make geometry legible and editable through language rather than a visual/mouse interface.

**Methodology summary:** A qualitative, single-arm exploratory field study (no baseline/control condition) with 4 blind/low-vision (BLV) participants, each completing 3 within-subjects sessions (~2.5 hrs each, spread over 11 days, ~7.5 hrs/participant total, $50/session). Session 1 = tutorial on OpenSCAD + A11yShape features plus simple standardized tasks; Session 2 = two guided tasks of increasing complexity (a "Tanghulu" model, then a standing robot or robot-trailer) built from natural-language descriptions with no visual reference, experimenters providing only OpenSCAD syntax help on request; Session 3 = free-form/self-selected creative modeling task to observe unstructured use. Data collected: think-aloud verbalizations throughout, semi-structured interviews after each task/session, full session recordings/transcripts, interaction logs (use of hierarchy navigation, AI verification loop, version control), a post-study System Usability Scale (SUS), and observational notes. Qualitative analysis used Braun & Clarke thematic analysis with two researchers independently open-coding line-by-line, iteratively building a shared hierarchical codebook, then independently re-coding all transcripts and resolving disagreements collaboratively (no numeric inter-rater reliability statistic reported). Quantitative interaction-log analysis was used only to triangulate/support the qualitative themes, not for inferential statistics. Separately, a companion validation study had 15 (sighted, presumably non-BLV) raters score AI-generated model descriptions on 5-point Likert scales across 5 dimensions (geometric accuracy, spatial relationships, clarity, completeness, hallucination-avoidance), reported as means/SDs only (no comparison condition or significance testing). AI backend: GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) with a multimodal prompting architecture combining OpenSCAD code, multi-angle rendered images (top/bottom/front/rear/left/right), and user queries; modeling backend is OpenSCAD; delivered as a WCAG-compliant, screen-reader-navigable Flask web app.

**Strengths:**
- Ecologically valid, longitudinal design: real BLV users doing genuinely open-ended, self-directed creative modeling tasks across multiple sessions (7.5 hrs/participant) rather than a single lab session with toy tasks, the free-form Session 3 is a nice touch reviewers tend to reward.
- Rigorous qualitative process: two independent coders, iterative codebook development, full re-coding pass with disagreements resolved collaboratively, a defensible thematic-analysis pipeline even without a numeric kappa/agreement statistic.
- Sensible, explicitly justified deviation from the standard 'baseline condition' convention: they argue existing 3D tools are literally unusable by screen-reader users, so a baseline comparison would be uninformative rather than illuminating, this is the kind of principled methodological call CHI/ASSETS reviewers respect when the population makes standard designs inapplicable.
- Triangulation across data types (think-aloud, interviews, interaction logs, SUS, artifact inspection) strengthens the qualitative claims beyond self-report alone.
- Honest limitations section that names concrete technical failure modes (LLM struggles with niche DSL syntax, no proactive error detection, undetected misalignment) rather than vague hedging, gives future work (including a related system) a clear methodological/technical precedent to cite.
- Separate, larger-N (15) Likert validation study of the AI-generated description quality is a good move to partially compensate for the small core-study N by giving a quantitative signal on a specific, isolable sub-component (description fidelity) rather than only relying on the 4-person field study for everything.

**Weaknesses:**
- Very small, non-diverse sample: N=4, all male, narrow age range (21-32), no claims of statistical generalizability are possible, and gender/background diversity within the BLV population is not represented.
- No control/baseline condition at all, and no counterbalancing of task order (Tanghulu always before robot task), this is defensible given the justification given, but it does mean any performance/difficulty differences across tasks are confounded with order and learning effects across the 3 sessions.
- No inter-rater reliability statistic (e.g., Cohen's kappa) reported for the thematic coding despite describing an independent double-coding process, reviewers focused on qualitative rigor sometimes want at least a qualitative account of disagreement rates.
- Recruitment explicitly biased toward BLV users who already had AI-chatbot familiarity and programming experience ('to reflect realistic use-case scenarios'), this likely inflates apparent usability/adoption success relative to the broader BLV population who may lack programming background, and the paper does not test how the tool performs for non-programmers.
- The 15-person Likert validation study of AI description quality is disconnected from the core BLV sample (unclear if these 15 raters were BLV or sighted, and whether they experienced the descriptions via the same non-visual modality as the real users), this weakens its relevance as evidence for the actual target population's experience.
- Task success is reported essentially as binary ('completed') without a shared objective quality/accuracy rubric for the resulting 3D models; the paper's own qualitative observations (misaligned propellers, structural conflicts) suggest 'completion' did not always mean geometrically correct output, so the completion metric may overstate quality.
- Self-reported measures (SUS, interviews) dominate; there is no independent/blinded assessment of model correctness or of participants' resulting spatial understanding (e.g., no test of whether participants could accurately describe/verify geometry unaided afterward).

**Sample size / conditions:** N=4 BLV participants (all male, ages 21-32, mean 24.3) for the core system study, 1 fully blind, 1 near-total light-perception-only, 2 with varying low vision; all screen-reader users, programming experience 1-10+ years, all had prior AI-chat familiarity, none had prior OpenSCAD/3D-modeling-tool experience. Single condition only (A11yShape itself); explicitly no baseline/control comparison against tools like Blender/Fusion 360, justified as "existing tools are effectively unusable with screen readers, making such comparisons uninformative." 3 sessions/participant (tutorial, 2 guided tasks, 1 free-form task), 12 total models produced. A separate, larger (N=15) rating study assessed AI-description quality on Likert scales, independent of the 4-person main study.

**Measures used:** Primary: System Usability Scale (SUS, mean=80.6); task completion/success (binary, all 4 completed all tasks); qualitative thematic codes from think-aloud + semi-structured interview transcripts (themes covering system reactions, code editor, AI responses, version control, hierarchical model representation, cross-representation highlighting, task performance/challenges, and improvement suggestions); interaction-log counts of feature use (hierarchy navigation, verification-loop invocations, version control) used descriptively to triangulate qualitative claims. Secondary/validation study: 5-point Likert ratings from 15 raters on 5 dimensions of AI-generated description quality (geometric accuracy, spatial relationships, clarity/comprehensibility, completeness, hallucination avoidance), reported as means and SDs with no inferential test.

**Supplementary material notes:** Appendix A contains the actual LLM prompts used for two distinct tasks: (A.1) generating natural-language descriptions of OpenSCAD models from code + multi-angle renders, and (A.2) comparing two code versions to summarize an edit/diff for the user, the paper notes this second task is treated as 'well-bounded and deterministic, making LLM-based summaries reliable,' which is a useful methodological framing (i.e., constrain the LLM to narrow, verifiable sub-tasks rather than open-ended judgment) that is directly reusable. The companion 15-rater Likert validation study (5 dimensions: geometric accuracy, spatial relationships, clarity, completeness, hallucination-avoidance) is effectively a separate, smaller supplementary study bolted onto the main qualitative field study.

**Cite in main paper:** Cite in the main related-work section: A11yShape is a directly analogous, very recent (ASSETS 2025) precedent for pairing an LLM agent with a parametric 3D-modeling backend (OpenSCAD) to make geometry legible and editable through natural language rather than a visual/mouse interface, exactly the "language as the interface to 3D geometry" move our tool needs for voice-first, non-expert co-design, making it essential company for our claim that AI-mediated natural-language access to 3D models is an emerging, validated design pattern (not just for accessibility, but for any population excluded from expert CAD interaction, including our non-expert community groups).

**Cite in supplementary:** Two methodological precedents worth logging in our supplementary materials: (1) their explicit, reasoned justification for omitting a baseline/control condition when the comparison tools are fundamentally inaccessible/unusable to the target population, a template argument we may need if our voice-first tool likewise has no meaningful 'mouse/keyboard baseline' for the non-expert community members we recruit; and (2) their prompt-engineering pattern of restricting LLM use to narrow, well-bounded, near-deterministic sub-tasks (e.g., diff-summarization between two geometry versions) specifically because open-ended geometric reasoning was unreliable, a concrete, citable limitation-and-mitigation precedent for how we scope what our own AI co-design agent is and isn't asked to judge autonomously.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- All four participants successfully completed both guided and free-form 3-D modeling tasks, producing 12 distinct models; mean SUS = 80.6.
- "I had never modeled before and never thought I could...through today's simple modeling process...we can indeed create relatively simple structures." (P4)
- P2 described the experience as "revolutionary" and called the editor "one of the most satisfying parts...very accessible to use."

- Explicit stated limitation: the system 'lacks proactive detection and correction mechanisms' and 'operates as a passive tool rather than an active agent that identifies potential problems.'

### "Put-That-There": Voice and Gesture at the Graphics Interface
- **Authors:** Richard A. Bolt
- **Venue/Year:** SIGGRAPH 1980 (ACM SIGGRAPH Computer Graphics) 1980
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/965105.807503
- **Relevance:** Foundational multimodal voice+gesture interface for direct manipulation of graphical objects on a large display; the canonical precedent motivating why voice paired with spatial reference/pointing is a natural modality for non-expert 3D scene manipulation.

**Methodology summary:** This is not an empirical study in the CHI sense, it is a systems/demonstration paper from early SIGGRAPH (1980) describing a working prototype (the MIT Architecture Machine Group's 'Media Room'). The 'methodology' consists of: (1) building an integrated architecture that fuses a connected-speech recognizer with a Polhemus magnetic position sensor worn on the user's wrist to track pointing gesture in real time; (2) a frame-based semantic parser that treats deictic words ('that', 'there') as slots to be filled by whatever object/location the simultaneous pointing gesture resolves to; (3) illustrating the system's behavior through a handful of scripted example dialogues/interaction transcripts (e.g., creating, naming, moving, and deleting colored shapes on a large-screen display) rather than through a controlled experiment. There is no described participant recruitment, no between/within-subjects conditions, no counterbalancing, and no quantitative outcome measures (completion time, error rate, subjective ratings) reported; the 'evaluation' is qualitative/anecdotal, oriented toward proving technical feasibility and illustrating a new interaction concept.

**Strengths:**
- Conceptual originality: introduced and named the 'put-that-there' multimodal deixis paradigm, combining natural speech with simultaneous pointing gesture to resolve referring expressions, which became the canonical founding example for multimodal interaction research for the next four decades.
- Real, working, integrated system (not merely a concept sketch): fused a contemporary connected-speech recognizer with a novel 6-DOF magnetic position sensor (Polhemus cube) in real time, which was a nontrivial systems/engineering achievement for 1980 hardware.
- Clear, generalizable interaction principle (temporal co-occurrence of speech slot-filling with pointing location) that is explainable independent of the specific hardware, letting later work reapply the idea to new modalities (touch, mouse, 3D controllers, VR/AR gesture) and new domains, exactly the kind of technical mechanism worth borrowing conceptually today.
- Historic/foundational status is itself a strength for citation purposes: it is undisputed as the origin point of the voice+gesture/pointing multimodal interface lineage, which gives it strong argumentative weight in a related-work section even without rigorous empirical backing.

**Weaknesses:**
- No controlled empirical evaluation whatsoever: no defined sample size, no participants beyond apparent informal/lab demonstrations, no experimental conditions, no quantitative outcome measures, and no statistical analysis, by modern CHI standards this would not pass methodological review as an empirical contribution (it would only be acceptable as a systems/technique paper).
- Findings are illustrated via a small number of scripted, seemingly hand-picked example transcripts rather than representative task trials, so there is no way to assess robustness, error rates, failure modes, or how often deictic resolution actually succeeded versus failed.
- Single-user, non-collaborative setting: the interaction is one person manipulating simple colored shapes; there is no test of multi-user co-manipulation, turn-taking, or conflicting simultaneous input, a real limitation for anyone (like us) wanting to cite it as precedent for *multi-user* voice+gesture co-design.
- Constrained, toy task domain: simple 2D colored geometric shapes / schematic map objects, not real-world complex 3D scenes or domain objects with zoning/architectural constraints, the deictic-resolution problem is far easier when there are only a few, well-separated, uniquely colored shapes on screen than in a cluttered real 3D architectural model.
- Hardware-tethered gesture capture (wrist-worn magnetic sensor, seated posture) is not naturalistic or scalable, and the paper does not discuss generalizability across users, individual differences in speech or gesture style, or accessibility.
- No discussion of failure/ambiguity handling at any depth, e.g., what happens with imprecise pointing, overlapping objects, or speech misrecognition, leaving a major open question that later multimodal-fusion literature (e.g., late 1980s 1990s work by Cohen, Oviatt) had to address empirically.

**Sample size / conditions:** No sample size, participant recruitment, or experimental conditions are reported. The demonstrations described are of the system operating with (apparently) the author/lab members as illustrative users; there is no evidence of a controlled multi-participant study, no between-subjects or within-subjects design, and no baseline/comparison condition (e.g., speech-only vs. gesture-only vs. combined) tested empirically.

**Measures used:** No formal quantitative measures (e.g., task completion time, error rate, recognition accuracy, System Usability Scale, NASA-TLX) are reported. The paper's central 'measure' is qualitative demonstration: whether the system could correctly resolve a deictic reference (e.g., 'that'/'there') to the correct on-screen object/location using the fused speech+point-timing input, shown via example transcripts.

**Supplementary material notes:** No separate supplementary materials or appendix exist for this 1980 SIGGRAPH paper (a short conference paper predating the modern CHI/SIGGRAPH supplementary-materials convention); the 'Media Room' system and Polhemus-based gesture tracking are documented only within the main text and the paper's own illustrative figures/transcripts. I was unable to run a live web search in this session (search budget exhausted) to pull the original PDF or check for any later-digitized ACM DL supplementary files, so this summary relies on well-established, high-confidence scholarly knowledge of this canonical, extensively-cited paper rather than a fresh primary-source re-read; if a verbatim quote or page-specific detail is needed for the manuscript, the primary source (ACM SIGGRAPH Computer Graphics, Vol. 14, No. 3, 1980, pp. 262-270, ACM Digital Library DOI) should be pulled directly before submission.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite it in the main paper's related-work/motivation section as the canonical origin of voice-plus-pointing multimodal direct manipulation, in one sentence establishing why pairing spoken command with spatial/gestural reference is a long-established, natural modality for non-expert manipulation of graphical (now 3D architectural) objects, while being explicit that it is cited as a foundational technical/conceptual precedent rather than as empirical evidence.

**Cite in supplementary:** Because Bolt (1980) itself supplies no empirical methodology to emulate, its main supplementary-relevant contribution is a cautionary methodological data point: it is the earliest example in this lineage of a multimodal system validated only by illustrative demonstration with no participants, conditions, or measures, and only single-user/toy-domain, worth a footnote in our supplementary materials as the historical baseline our study explicitly improves on by running a real multi-participant, multi-user, real-task (zoning-compliant design) evaluation with defined measures, rather than an anecdotal demo.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Bolt's own framing: the system lets a user "create and move objects on a large-screen graphics display by a combination of speech and pointing," using the eponymous example utterance "Put that there" where 'that' and 'there' are deictic terms resolved via simultaneous gesture tracking.
- The Media Room setup: user seated in a chair facing a 12-foot rear-projected display, wearing a Polhemus cube (a wrist-mounted magnetic 6-DOF position/orientation sensor) so pointing direction/location could be captured continuously and fused with a connected-speech recognizer's parsed command frame.
- The paper reports the system working on a simple 'graphics scene' of colored geometric shapes (and a schematic map-like world with objects such as ships), with commands like 'Create a blue square there', 'Move that to the right of the green square', and 'Delete that', demonstrated via illustrative transcripts/vignettes rather than a controlled experiment.
- No formal participant sample, condition manipulation, quantitative task measure (e.g., time, errors, accuracy), or statistical analysis appears in the paper, it is presented and has been treated by the HCI community ever since as a systems/technology-demonstration paper, not an empirical user study.

### WordsEye: An Automatic Text-to-Scene Conversion System
- **Authors:** Bob Coyne, Richard Sproat
- **Venue/Year:** SIGGRAPH 2001 2001
- **URL:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/383259.383316
- **Relevance:** Foundational natural-language-to-3D-scene generation system converting textual descriptions into populated 3D scenes; grounds the natural-language-to-scene generation lineage that LLM-agent scene editing in the proposed tool builds on.

**Methodology summary:** The 2001 SIGGRAPH paper itself contains NO empirical user study, evaluation, sample, or statistical analysis, it is a systems/engineering paper. Its "methodology" is a pipeline architecture description validated only by illustrative worked examples (e.g., "John rides the horse by the store..."): (1) POS-tag and statistically parse input text (Collins parser, trained on Penn Treebank); (2) convert parse tree to a dependency structure; (3) semantically interpret dependencies into a semantic representation (entities/actions/attributes/relations) using WordNet for noun hypernym/hyponym lookup and hand-built verb frames (~1300 nouns, ~2300 verbs); (4) resolve anaphora/coreference heuristically via WordNet hierarchy matching; (5) apply hand-authored "depiction rules" that map semantic elements to low-level "depictors" (3D object choice, pose, spatial relation, color/size/transparency), using per-object metadata (skeletons, spatial tags like "cup"/"canopy area", functional properties, parts) manually curated for ~2000 3D objects; (6) resolve implicit constraints (commonsense defaults) and conflicting constraints (tentative vs. explicit depictors) via transduction rules; (7) apply depictors in a fixed priority order (size/shape → pose/attachment → position → orientation → paths/IK); (8) auto-frame camera and render. Validation throughout is by demonstration/case example, not by held-out test set, user study, or quantitative metric. The paper explicitly frames itself as a first system description with acknowledged future work, not an evaluation.

Because the target paper has no empirical study design, I also pulled the follow-up paper that supplies one: Ulinski, Coyne & Hirschberg, "Evaluating the WordsEye Text-to-Scene System: Imaginative and Realistic Sentences" (LREC 2018), which conducts the kind of study a CHI reviewer would expect and is the natural methodological companion/precedent.

**Strengths:**
- Deep, well-motivated system design: each depiction mechanism (spatial tags, poses/grips, IK, implicit/conflicting-constraint transduction rules) is grounded in a real linguistic/semantics literature (WordNet, Levin verb classes, Cognitive Grammar, spatial-preposition semantics) rather than ad hoc heuristics, gives the paper unusual explanatory depth for a systems paper.
- Honest, explicit acknowledgment of scope limits and unsolved cases (degeneralization, environments/activities, materials/textures) rather than overclaiming, a trait CHI/SIGGRAPH reviewers reward.
- The core representational idea (semantic elements -> depiction rules -> low-level depictors, with tentative vs. explicit constraint resolution) is a genuinely reusable architectural pattern that later systems (and modern LLM scene-graph agents) still echo.
- The companion 2018 evaluation paper (same research group) is methodologically solid for its era: two contrasting, purpose-built corpora (crowdsourced imaginative vs. corpus-derived realistic) let them test an explicit hypothesis (system strength varies by content type) rather than a single aggregate score; uses both a forced-choice and an independent graded-rating measure and cross-tabulates them, which is more diagnostic than either alone; includes a structured, module-level error analysis tied back to the pipeline architecture, making failures actionable rather than just reported.

**Weaknesses:**
- The target paper (SIGGRAPH 2001) has literally no evaluation: no user study, no baseline comparison, no quantitative measure, no sample size, a reviewer applying 2020s-era CHI standards would flag this immediately as non-empirical; it survives on novelty and demonstration alone, which was acceptable for SIGGRAPH systems papers in 2001 but would not pass a CHI empirical-contribution bar today.
- Object/pose/depiction-rule coverage is entirely hand-authored and hand-curated (skeletons, spatial tags, grips, functional properties per object), this is a scalability and generalization weakness the authors partly concede ("vocabulary is... limited only by what we are able to depict") but never quantify (no coverage rate, no failure rate on unseen text).
- Entity-to-3D-object selection is stated to be done 'randomly among those matching the given term' with no justification or evaluation of that design choice, and pronoun/noun coreference resolution is a crude WordNet-hierarchy heuristic acknowledged to only 'guess' at correct antecedents.
- In the companion 2018 evaluation (the paper that does have a design), the realistic-sentence subsampling is a real construct-validity threat: only ~1/3 of PASCAL captions could be depicted at all, and hard cases (action verbs, unrepresented nouns) were filtered out before evaluation, so the comparison against Google Image Search is on an easier subset favorable to WordsEye's coverage, which likely inflates its apparent competitiveness.
- AMT rater qualifications (98% approval, 100-500 prior HITs) and $0.01-0.04 payment are typical of the period but provide no inter-rater reliability statistic (no Cohen's kappa/ICC reported) despite averaging only 3-5 raters per item, and no significance testing (e.g., no reported p-values or CIs on the vote/rating differences) is provided anywhere in the results section.

**Sample size / conditions:** Main 2001 paper: N/A, no participants, no conditions, no dataset; only a handful of authored example sentences/figures used to illustrate each mechanism (e.g., kick, push, spatial-tag examples).

Companion 2018 evaluation paper (relevant precedent for supplementary material): Two sentence sets, 209 crowdsourced "imaginative" sentences (built from AMT workers given word lists spanning 11 lexical categories: PROP, FIXTURE, ANIMAL, SPATIAL TERM, NUMBER, COLOR, SIZE, DISTANCE, SURFACE PROPERTY, LOCATION, BUILDING; 12 category-combination templates × 20 HITs, filtered from 240 collected down to 209 grammatical ones) and 250 "realistic" sentences (filtered from the 5,000-sentence PASCAL image-caption corpus, keeping ~1/3 that WordsEye's lexicon/parser could handle). Conditions: for each sentence, best-of-4 Google Image Search results vs. best-of-4 auto-generated WordsEye renderings (varied camera angle/elevation), each selected via 5 AMT judgments (first phase) then compared head-to-head via 3 AMT judgments (second phase, no ties possible) plus a separate 1 to 5 correctness-rating task (3 judgments, averaged). AMT worker qualification: ≥100 (collection) or ≥500 (rating) prior HITs, ≥98% approval; paid $0.01 $0.04/HIT.

**Measures used:** Main 2001 paper: none (no dependent measures, purely descriptive/demonstrative).

2018 companion paper: (1) binary forced-choice votes (WordsEye image vs. Google image, majority of 3 AMT judges) tabulated by sentence category; (2) a 1 to 5 Likert-style "illustration correctness" rating (1 = completely correct, 5 = completely incorrect) averaged over 3 raters per image; (3) a post-hoc qualitative error taxonomy coded by two of the authors against the 8 pipeline modules (Knowledge Base, Graphics Library, Parsing, Reference Resolution, Semantic Analysis, Graphical Analysis, Apply Graphical Constraints, Camera/Render) applied to every image rated worse than 2.

**Supplementary material notes:** The 2001 paper itself contributes no reusable measure or limitation worth citing methodologically (it has no study to draw from). Instead, pull the methodological precedent from its own follow-up, Ulinski, Coyne & Hirschberg (LREC 2018) "Evaluating the WordsEye Text-to-Scene System": specifically (a) their two-track evaluation design, separately testing system performance on crowdsourced *imaginative* prompts vs. corpus-derived *realistic* prompts, as a precedent for us to evaluate our own LLM-to-3D co-design tool separately on 'creative/aspirational' community design prompts vs. 'realistic/code-constrained' zoning prompts, since performance may likewise diverge by prompt type; and (b) their module-level error taxonomy (knowledge-base / object-library / parsing / reference-resolution / semantic-analysis / graphical-analysis / constraint-application / camera-render) as a template for a failure-mode breakdown table in our own supplementary materials when reporting where our LLM-agent scene edits go wrong. Also worth noting as a limitation-to-preempt: their finding that a forced-choice preference vote and a continuous quality rating can diverge (WordsEye won votes even when rated numerically worse), if our supplementary evaluation uses both a preference measure and a quality rating, we should expect and report this same kind of divergence rather than being surprised by it.

**Cite in main paper:** Yes, cite in the MAIN paper as the foundational, most-cited origin point of the natural-language-to-3D-scene-generation lineage (rule-based semantic-frame → depiction-rule pipeline) that any LLM-agent-driven scene/architecture editing system, including ours, is a direct intellectual descendant of, even though its methods (hand-authored WordNet/verb-frame rules) are now superseded by LLM-based semantic parsing.

**Key findings/quotes:**
- Main paper self-description: "WordsEye is currently a research project and is under active development", explicitly a systems demo, not an evaluated study.
- Main paper acknowledges its own core limitation directly: "Since linguistic descriptions tend to be at a high level of abstraction, there will be a certain amount of unpredictability in the graphical result... it is infeasible to fully capture the semantic content of language in graphics."
- Companion 2018 paper key result: WordsEye pictures rated 2.581 (imaginative) vs. 2.536 (realistic), essentially flat across sentence type; Google rated 3.825 (imaginative, much worse) vs. 1.871 (realistic, much better).
- Companion paper: on imaginative sentences, WordsEye won the binary vote 85.6% of the time even in the 42 cases where Google's continuous rating was numerically better (won 24/42 of those by vote), a documented gap between forced-choice preference and graded quality rating.
- Companion paper's error taxonomy is a directly reusable methodological artifact: Camera/Render errors dominated on imaginative sentences (42.1%) while Semantic Analysis (15.2%), Graphical Analysis (25.6%), and Apply-Graphical-Constraints (21.2%) errors dominated on realistic sentences, i.e., failure modes differ systematically by sentence type, a pattern worth anticipating in any LLM-to-3D-scene pipeline.

## CHI methodological rigor (unrelated domains)

- **Observations on Typing from 136 Million Keystrokes**, Vivek Dhakal, Anna Feit, Per Ola Kristensson, Antti Oulasvirta, ACM CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI 2018) 2018. Domain: typing/input performance (not civic design). - **When (ish) is My Bus? User-centered Visualizations of Uncertainty in Everyday, Mobile Predictive Systems**, Matthew Kay, Tara Kola, Jessica Hullman, Sean A. - **Activity Sensing in the Wild: A Field Trial of UbiFit Garden**, Sunny Consolvo, David W. McDonald, Tammy Toscos, Mike Y.
